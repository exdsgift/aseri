# Say it once, then let pixels count

aseri's statistics are sound. How the page delivers them is the problem. Its statistical choices match the evidence: ranges before point values, frequencies instead of percentages, the complement "2 in 10 outside", and a backtest kept apart from the live record. But the page states the GPR forecast four times, and it uses "8 in 10" 23 times as a label without saying 8 in 10 *of what*. It spends about 906 visible tokens where 250 to 300 would carry every fact a first-time reader needs. Two displays mislead before any of that text is read. The hero sets a 7-day average (181) beside a one-day forecast ("most likely 168"), so readers infer a fall, while the like-for-like value (last Monday, 165) says the outlook is flat. The panel opens on a single drawn path labelled "the most likely". Running the site's own path code shows that this path still spends 35 to 38% of its days outside the middle half of the range, and crosses the middle on 41 to 55% of days. Its bumps are random, but they read as predicted events. The fix is to say each idea once, as a sentence that names what it counts, and let pixel marks do the counting. That means a takeaway title on every block, computed from the data ("Likely 110-250 by Nov 23, about where it is now"), one label on the band ("8 in 10 days land here", "2 in 10 outside"), a labelled "normal" line, a 20-block stack of possible values on the target day, and the Record's squares promoted to a trust headline ("33 of 40 landed in range"). The evidence backs half of the owner's path decisions. Animated draws beat static intervals and line ensembles for lay readers, and leaving out the median follows the Bank of England. But a frozen opening path, and a replay that measures the real line against a path, both judge the forecast by the wrong test. Each has a fix that keeps the paths. None of the new wording has been tested on aseri's readers, so a five-second test and three comprehension probes should gate the rewrite.

## The page states its forecast four times and defines it once, under the chart

I measured the rendered page of 2026-10-31, with five series live. aseri shows about **906 visible text tokens**: 61 in the hero, 297 in the forecast panel, 166 in Signals, 346 in the Record and 35 in the footer and side navigation ([live site](https://exdsgift.github.io/aseri/)). That is about 4.3 screens at 1440x900 and 6.6 screens on a 390 px phone. The same GPR forecast ("111-251", "most likely 168") appears four times. **"8 in 10" appears 23 times**, "most likely" 8 times and "7-day average" 7 times. "Collecting live record: 0 of 30" and "Backtest, not the live record" appear five times each, once per Record card ([TrackRecord.tsx](../../../web/src/components/TrackRecord.tsx)). The one sentence that defines the band ("where the daily value should land 8 days in 10, outside it about 2 in 10") is the second paragraph under the chart, after a seven-item legend ([ForecastPanel.tsx](../../../web/src/components/ForecastPanel.tsx)).

Volume matters because almost nobody reads it. In Nielsen's tests, **79% of users scanned any new page and only 16% read word by word**. Text that was concise, scannable and objective, written conclusion-first in half the words or fewer, scored 124% better in usability ([NN/g, How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)). Readers fall into F-shaped scanning when nothing pulls the eye toward meaning, so each heading should start with the words that carry its gist ([NN/g, F-Shaped Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). aseri's blocks start with metadata instead. The panel opens with "[F] Geopolitical risk · forecast made Oct 30", "index (1985-2019 average = 100) · data to Oct 26" and "Data as of Oct 31, preliminary for about 4 weeks." A reader who takes only the first words of each line collects dates, not conclusions. At least ten distinct dates appear in the first two screens. The hero's eyebrow says "today" above a value that is an average to Oct 26. Every secondary string is set in 8 or 12 px uppercase pixel type (Press Start 2P, DotGothic16). Datawrapper's floor for chart text is 12 px in sentence case ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations)). No source in the notes measures pixel-font legibility at 8 px, so the extra cost per word is an expert judgement, but it makes every cut worth more.

Here, repetition does not reinforce the message: it wears it out. Used 23 times as a noun ("8 in 10: 111-251", "the 8 in 10 band", "Inside the 8 in 10 range"), "8 in 10" stops being frequency framing and becomes a code to decode: "80% PI" in friendlier clothes. The table sets what each block should tell a newcomer in five seconds against what it tells today.

| Block | Should say in five seconds | Says today |
|---|---|---|
| Hero | Risk is 81% above normal and should stay about this high through Nov 23 | "181, 81% above", then a 27-word sentence that reads as "falling to 168" |
| Forecast panel | Where it has been, and the range it should stay in through Nov 23 | Six lines of dates and metadata, a chart whose only line ahead looks like the prediction, 7 legend items, 72 words of explanation |
| Signals | Which lanes are abnormal now, and which way they are heading | Five cards of equal weight. The page's largest anomaly (Hormuz, 3 ships a day, 88% below its mean) sits in 8 px grey |
| Record | Can I trust these ranges? 33 of the last 40 landed in range, close to the 8 in 10 promised | "Collecting live record: 0 of 30" five times, then ratio tables with 95% brackets. No total anywhere |

Two facts set the urgency. First, the page will grow. Each new series adds about 30 tokens of card and 70 of Record, and [Signals.tsx](../../../web/src/components/Signals.tsx) already defines nine series against the five live today. The per-card repetition should be fixed before more series go live. Second, much of what is there is right. The range comes before the point value, the complement is stated, words back every marker, the backtest is kept honestly apart from the live record, and the Record squares open the replay. The redesign should change how the page delivers its forecasts, not its statistical framing.

## Two displays mislead before a word is read

### The hero sets a weekly average against a one-day forecast

The giant number, 181, is GPR's 7-day average to Oct 26. The sentence under it forecasts the *daily* value on Monday Nov 23: "8 in 10 between 111 and 251, 2 in 10 outside. Most likely 168. (Last Monday: 165)" ([Hero.tsx](../../../web/src/components/Hero.tsx)). A reader subtracts 168 from 181 and concludes that risk should fall by about 13 points. The only like-for-like figure is last Monday's 165, which comes last and in brackets. Against it, the outlook is flat to slightly up. People who forecast from graphs anchor on the most salient recent value and under-adjust ([Harvey and Reimers 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/13591)), and here the most salient value is the wrong quantity. The same mismatch repeats on every card: Suez shows "41 ships a day", a 7-day average, above "In 28 days: 33-51", a one-day range ([SignalCard.tsx](../../../web/src/components/SignalCard.tsx)). **This is the most damaging problem on the page, because it gives the reader a confident wrong answer, not just a slow right one.** The fix has two parts. Label the big number for what it is ("7-day average to Oct 26"). Then anchor the outlook on the like-for-like value, in words ("about where it is now") and in the graphic (a "last Monday 165" marker on the range bar).

### The opening path is noise drawn to look like a prediction

The 20 paths are not invented. The pipeline estimates each series' day-to-day tie: the lag-1 correlation of where real days fell inside past forecasts ([tie.py](../../../pipeline/aseri/tie.py)). The page draws each path from the published quantiles through a Gaussian AR(1) process with that tie. It then ranks the 20 paths by the sum of their days' squared normal scores ([paths.ts](../../../web/src/lib/paths.ts)). On 2026-10-31 the ties were 0.45 for GPR, 0.30 for Bab el-Mandeb, 0.14 for Taiwan, 0.08 for Hormuz and -0.26 for Suez ([live catalog](https://exdsgift.github.io/aseri/api/v1/series.json)). That is a sound way to draw possible futures together. The trouble is what the first frame says. Under this model, the single most likely path is the middle of the band on every day. The "most likely" path on screen is only the most typical of 20 random draws.

I ran the site's algorithm on 20,000 simulated 28-day forecasts for each live tie. **Path 1 spends 35% (GPR) to 38% of its days outside the middle half of the range and 7 to 9% outside the 8-in-10 band. It crosses from one side of the middle to the other on 41% (GPR) to 55% (Suez) of days.** Every bump it makes inside the band is random. The weekly rhythm, which is real, is already in the band's own shape. Yet a lone line with a Monday dip and a Thursday peak looks exactly like a forecast of a Monday dip and a Thursday peak. The code admits as much: "Drawn from the forecast, a path reads like data: the words say what it is". The words that say what it is are a 27-word paragraph under the chart ([ForecastPanel.tsx](../../../web/src/components/ForecastPanel.tsx)).

The literature predicts the misreading. Viewers "interpret the image as representing some deterministic quantity". In Savelli and Joslyn's experiments, a prominent key, broken lines and connecting lines "did not reliably prevent the error", and the authors advise against emphasising particular points ([Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full)). With ensemble displays, people overweight the individual members that pass near a point of interest ([Padilla et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-017-0076-1)). The mini charts on the cards draw the same path 1 at card size, with no words at all ([SignalCard.tsx](../../../web/src/components/SignalCard.tsx)).

### Where the evidence backs the path decisions, and where it pushes back

Much of the evidence is on the owner's side. Leaving out a median line follows the fan chart's inventor. The Bank of England designed the fan chart "to focus attention on the whole of the forecast distribution, rather than on small changes to the central projection" ([BoE Quarterly Bulletin 1998](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/1998/q1/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart)), and it "has always refused to include a numerical central estimate when first releasing the graphic" ([Spiegelhalter, Pearson and Short 2011](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/spiegelhalter_visualizing.pdf)). Adding a mean to an uncertainty display biases judgments, because users then discount the uncertainty ([Kale, Kay and Hullman 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14516)). Play makes a hypothetical outcome plot (HOP), one of the best-tested formats. In comparisons, HOPs gave much lower error than error bars and violin plots ([Hullman, Resnick and Adar 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0142444)). For time series, untrained observers identified the true trend from weaker evidence with HOPs than with error bars or **line ensembles** ([Kale et al. 2019](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops-trends)). That supports the owner's rejection of 20 overlapping lines. The strongest case for a single opening path is trust. In Padilla's COVID experiments, displays with less information, including a single forecast, earned the most trust ([Padilla et al. 2023](https://vis.khoury.northeastern.edu/pubs/Padilla2022MultipleForecastVisualizations)). In the Bank of England's 2026 experiment, the public rated point forecasts the easiest format, 4.9 out of 7 ([McMahon et al., BoE SWP 1196](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty.pdf)).

The same study shows the price. For point-forecast readers, forecast errors "significantly de-anchor expectations". Fan charts "materially mitigate this de-anchoring, acting as an 'insurance policy'" ([BoE SWP 1196](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty.pdf)). A static path 1 is not a HOP. HOPs work through many draws seen over time. One frozen draw is a point forecast with noise added: it brings back the central line the owner removed, plus spikes. **The evidence therefore conflicts with opening on a frozen path called "the most likely".**

The least disruptive fix keeps the decision and changes five details:

1. Rename the path, on the line itself, "1 of 20 possible paths", never "the most likely".
2. Draw it thinner and dimmer than the history and the band's labelled edges, so the band reads first.
3. When the panel first scrolls into view, play the 20 paths once and stop on path 1, so every visitor sees the HOP, not only those who press Play. Under reduced motion, do not autoplay. Keep the pace slow and play the sequence only once: motion for its own sake backfires. The New York Times needle's jitter was dropped after 2016 ([CJR](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/2018-midterms-forecasts-538-cnn-times-needle.php)) and was later called "the most hated data visualization in politics" ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90459366/the-most-hated-data-visualization-in-politics-is-back-to-spike-your-blood-pressure)).
4. Stack the 20 possible values for the target day as blocks at the chart's right edge (see the next section), so path 1 is visibly one block among twenty.
5. On the cards, draw the band and the normal line. If the path stays, give it the band's visual weight.

A pixel-native option, untested, is a short ghost trail during Play: the last two or three paths fade one dither step at a time. The eye then sees a crowd of futures without the spaghetti the owner rejected.

The replay decision has a sharper problem. When the real values become a line set against the model's line, the reader judges the forecast by the gap between the two lines. But the forecast claims the band, not the path. If the forecast is perfectly calibrated, the real outcome and a drawn path are two independent draws from the same range. Their typical gap is then about **1.4 times** the typical gap between the real value and the middle of the band: the square root of two. The factor is smaller for path 1, which stays near the middle, but still above one. An honest model will look wrong in every replay.

People who watch an algorithm err lose confidence in it, and they bet on a worse human forecaster instead, even after seeing the algorithm win ([Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey 2015](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/20-Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2015.pdf)). The uncertainty band is what restores credibility after a miss ([BoE SWP 1196](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty.pdf)). The Hubverse reference plot sets observed values against the forecast's shaded bands, not against a single draw ([hubVis](https://hubverse-org.github.io/hubVis/reference/plot_step_ahead_model_output.html)). The least disruptive fix keeps both lines and moves the verdict to the band:

- The real line becomes the dominant mark, and the path steps back.
- A filled or hollow square under each resolved day shows "in range" or "outside".
- The readout counts the squares: "■■■□□ 3 of 5 days in range".

One untested line of copy can turn the comparison into a lesson: "What happened is one more path. 8 in 10 days should land in the shaded range." The replay should also say which data vintage the real line shows, and put "Backtest" in its title when the forecast was made after the fact.

## Say "8 in 10" once, and say what it counts

The format "8 in 10" is not the mistake. A living review of 327 studies finds "no consistent benefits to percentage or frequency formats". It warns against "1 in X" phrasing, which inflates perceived probability, and it finds that naming the reference class improves interpretation ([Ripberger et al. 2022](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/60526/noaa_60526_DS1.pdf)). The reference class (8 in 10 *of what*) is the missing half. Gigerenzer and colleagues asked people in five cities what "30% chance of rain tomorrow" means. Only in New York did most give the meteorological reading; elsewhere people read it as 30% of the time or 30% of the area ([Gigerenzer et al. 2005](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/16975)). The fix is to say once what is counted, on the band itself: **"8 in 10 days land here"**. Days are the right reference class for aseri, because the replay and the Record already count days and forecasts, so readers can check the claim on the same page. Then show the other side. Stating both outcomes ("70% chance of rain and 30% chance of no rain") reduced misreadings ([Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full)). A short "↑↓ 2 in 10 outside" at the band's edge does this without a clause in every sentence. It also keeps one denominator of ten across the site, as Spiegelhalter advises ([Spiegelhalter et al. 2011](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/spiegelhalter_visualizing.pdf)).

Everywhere else, the word "likely" can carry the meaning, as long as the number travels with it. Readers give very different probabilities to the same word. About 1,700 people surveyed by Mauboussin and Mauboussin spread widely on "likely" ([FlowingData](https://flowingdata.com/2018/07/06/how-people-interpret-probability-through-words)). Students read the IPCC's "very likely" (over 90%) as 65 to 75% ([Full Fact briefing](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/en-communicating-uncertainty.pdf)). Printing the number in the text fixes most of this. With the US intelligence community's probability words, readers' ranges overlapped the official ones **66% of the time with numbers in brackets, 40% with a tooltip, 39% with a linked table and 32% with words alone**, and only about 46% of tooltip users ever opened the tooltip ([Wintle et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0213522)). The review's own example is "severe thunderstorms are possible (20% chance) this evening" ([Ripberger et al. 2022](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/60526/noaa_60526_DS1.pdf)).

Numbers do not cost trust. In the Winton Centre experiments, with about 5,780 UK participants including a BBC field test, a numeric range left trust in the number and its source unchanged, while a vague verbal caveat lowered it ([Full Fact briefing](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/en-communicating-uncertainty.pdf)). National-security professionals given precise probabilities became more cautious, not overconfident ([Friedman, Lerner and Zeckhauser 2017](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/behavioral_consequences.pdf)). The house format follows: **"likely 110-250 (8 in 10)"**, with the bracket inline and never in a tooltip. Rounding headline ranges to the nearest ten follows Few's warning against needless precision ([Few 2006](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)). No study tests rounding itself, so the chart labels keep the exact values.

"5 in 10" does not belong in the default view. It adds a second band whose dither is barely distinguishable from the outer band's at pixel scale, plus four more numbers on every interval row. Its visual job, showing that the middle is likelier than the edges, is real. The BoE's 2026 experiment found two-band fan charts "perform best overall" ([BoE SWP 1196](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty.pdf)). Gradient shading also avoids the hard inside-or-outside reading that sharp edges create ([Correll and Gleicher 2014](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf)). Yet in the Winton experiments, no format got people to see that central values are likelier than edge values ([Full Fact briefing](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/en-communicating-uncertainty.pdf)). So keep the inner density as an unlabelled gradient if the two densities can still be told apart in a grayscale check. Move its words to the details layer as "middle half: 143-207". Let the 20-block stack carry the "middle is likelier" message: the blocks pile up in the middle by construction.

"Most likely" is the riskiest phrase on the page, for three reasons:

- **It labels two different things:** the median ("most likely 168", the 0.5 quantile) and path 1 ("Path 1 of 20, the most likely") ([ForecastPanel.tsx](../../../web/src/components/ForecastPanel.tsx); [Intervals.tsx](../../../web/src/components/Intervals.tsx)).
- **It is loose for the median.** A range of 111 to 251 around 168 is lopsided, so the median is not the single most likely value (the mode).
- **It is rendered bold and bright**, against the advice not to emphasise medians.

Call the median "middle estimate, about 170". Show it only in the hover tip and the details table, and never make it bolder than the range. Call the path "1 of 20 possible paths". In the hero, replace the median with a direction word computed from the forecast. First take the like-for-like reference: last Monday for weekly GPR, the last value for the other series. Then read from the quantiles the chance that the target day lands above it. Above 0.65, the hero says "likely higher than now"; below 0.35, "likely lower than now"; in between, "about where it is now". These thresholds are a design choice, not a sourced rule. For GPR on Oct 31, a median of 168 against 165 gives "about where it is now".

The Record's vocabulary fails a lay reader twice. First, "Beats 0.77 (0.65-0.91)" is a ratio: the model's average miss divided by a simple method's. Lower is better, but "beats" next to a number reads as a score where higher wins ([ScoreTable.tsx](../../../web/src/components/ScoreTable.tsx)). Second, "Error vs 28-day mean" uses a word ("mean") that BBC audience research found to be a particular stumbling block ([Full Fact briefing](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/en-communicating-uncertainty.pdf)). No study in the notes tests whether lay readers understand beats, ties or loses against a baseline. The format with the least vocabulary is the Met Office's: the share of forecasts accurate within a stated tolerance, by how far ahead they were made. Its 1-day maximum-temperature forecasts were 95% accurate and its 5-day forecasts 77% ([Met Office](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/who-we-are/accuracy)). aseri's coverage figure already follows that pattern once it shows its target.

Keep the site's decision rule: "better" only when the whole interval sits below 1, "worse" only when it sits above, a tie otherwise. Change the words: name the simple method in plain language, and state the difference as a size. Pair every display of misses with its expected rate. Simple binomial arithmetic shows that at 30 honest forecasts, anything from about 66% to 94% in range is consistent with an 80% band. The legend "about 2 in 10 squares should be hollow if the range is honest" stops 8 of 8 from reading as a triumph and 6 of 8 as a failure.

## Pixel blocks can count what paragraphs explain

Every block needs a title that states its takeaway, computed from the data each day. Knaflic's standard example replaces the label "Retention" with "Headcount has increased 16% year-over-year" ([SHRM interview with Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/news/technology/using-pictures-storytelling-to-present-business-data)). The UK Office for National Statistics (ONS) asks for titles that are front-loaded, active and **15 words or fewer**, and that "describe the main trend you want the chart to show". If the trend in the title is not the most visible one in the chart, the guidance says to change the data or the chart ([ONS, Chart text](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/chart-text)). Datawrapper adds: lead with the plain takeaway, and keep jargon such as "median" out of titles ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations)). Apple Weather titles its next-hour rain chart so people get the key information "without requiring them to examine the details of the chart" ([Apple HIG, Charts](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charts)). For aseri:

- **Panel:** "Likely 110-250 by Nov 23, about where it is now"
- **Signals:** "Hormuz traffic 88% below normal; other lanes within 20%"
- **Record:** "Backtest: 33 of 40 forecasts landed in range, close to the 8 in 10 promised"

Each title is a template with three or four slots filled from JSON the site already publishes.

The chart can then lose its legend and its two paragraphs. Datawrapper removes the colour key and labels lines directly, to cut eye travel, and the World Bank style guide keeps legends for cases where direct labels cannot work ([World Bank style guide](https://worldbank.github.io/data-visualization-style-guide/chartelements)). Line-end labels "daily" and "7-day avg" replace two swatches, and a label on the dotted "now" line ("data to Oct 26") replaces a metadata line. The chart's biggest missing piece is its reference. The hero says "81% above normal", but the chart draws no line at 100. On desktop it shows a single y tick ("200"), so the claim has no visual. A number without a comparison is Few's second dashboard pitfall: "Compared to what? Is this good or bad?" ([Few 2006](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)). The Financial Times (FT) chart guide files variation from a long-term average under its own "deviation" category ([FT Visual Vocabulary](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)). A 1-px dashed "normal 100" line doubles as the missing second tick. The schematic below shows the default panel with these labels and the outcome stack. It is not to scale, and the path is left out for clarity.

```

**The 20-block stack on the right is the strongest addition the evidence supports, and it is native to pixel art.** It is a quantile dotplot: 20 dots, each worth 1 in 20. In the bus-arrival study, a 20-dot dotplot gave about **1.15 times lower variance** in probability estimates than a density plot. With 100 dots the gain vanished, because people stop counting and start estimating area ([Kay et al. 2016](https://www.mjskay.com/papers/chi_2016_uncertain_bus.pdf)). In an incentivised decision task, dotplots and cumulative-probability charts (CDFs) performed best, with 50-dot plots reaching about **97% of the optimal payoff** ([Fernandes et al. 2018](https://www.mjskay.com/papers/chi2018-uncertain-bus-decisions.pdf)). A public governor-race forecast used the same device with a one-line legend: "1 ball = 1 election outcome" ([Yang et al. 2024](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2023-US_midterm.pdf)).

For aseri, place one block at each of the 20 quantile levels from 0.025 to 0.975, interpolated from the published levels and binned to pixel rows, stacked sideways at the target day. By construction, 16 blocks land inside the band and 2 on each side. That makes "8 in 10" countable, and it piles the blocks up in the middle. Do not use the ends of the 20 random paths instead: their count inside the band would vary from day to day and contradict the label. The block nearest the played path's last value can light up during Play, which ties the animation to the count; that link is untested.

The hero needs the same move at a smaller scale: a range bar instead of a sentence. NN/g notes that gauges "consume a lot of precious space" and recommends linear bullet charts ([NN/g, Dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)). A horizontal bar also aligns with the pixel grid. Text labels stay on the bar, because the same interval was read correctly by 94% of people as text but misread by 36% as a graphic ([Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full)). The bar also shows something no sentence on the page says today: the normal tick at 100 sits left of the band's low end at 111. Even the low end of the likely range is above normal, and the reader sees it without a word.

The cards need one fixed anatomy, the same for all nine series. Few, IBM Carbon and Apple converge on the same parts:

- a short label;
- one value at the precision the task needs;
- one change against a named reference;
- a state shown by shape, not colour alone;
- a full-width small chart;
- at most one sentence.

Sources: [Few 2006](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf); [IBM Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/dashboards/); [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charts). NN/g says colour "should only be used to reinforce information that is already communicated in a different way" ([NN/g, Dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)). WCAG forbids colour as the only cue ([W3C, SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). So the state is a chip with a pixel arrow and a number: "▼ 88% vs normal". The mini chart gets a dashed normal line, and the gap to it is filled as columns above or below, the FT's surplus-and-deficit chart, which pixel art draws naturally.

Two smaller rules complete the cards. Every card should use the same forecast horizon, so cards can be compared. And the cards should keep their fixed order: a daily forecast is a monitoring task, and repeat visitors need the same positions every day ([NN/g, Dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)). Flag unusual cards instead of reordering them. One rule, which is a proposal: flag a card when its 7-day value sits outside its own 8-in-10 range of the past year.

3 ships a day · 7-day avg

Nov 08: likely 0-6 a day
```

The Record already holds the page's best graphic. Its squares are an icon array: filled when the real value landed in range, hollow when it missed. "Many studies show that [icon arrays] can increase risk comprehension", and they help low-numeracy readers in particular ([Ripberger et al. 2022](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/60526/noaa_60526_DS1.pdf)). The squares need three additions:

- a total line above them, which becomes the section title;
- a key shown once ("■ landed in range □ missed");
- the expected-rate line ("about 2 in 10 should be hollow if the range is honest").

Coverage in the details layer becomes a ten-block bar with a tick at 8, so "78% · aim 80%" is a picture before it is a number. FiveThirtyEight's long-running "Checking our work" page set the precedent of publishing calibration openly, including when results were off ([FiveThirtyEight](https://projects.fivethirtyeight.com/checking-our-work)).

The Intervals block should fold into the chart. Its 7, 14 and 28-day rows repeat the readout and the hero, and the chart's hover tip already shows the range, the middle value and what happened for any day ([PixelForecastChart.tsx](../../../web/src/components/PixelForecastChart.tsx)). Its lanes put the value on the x axis, a second chart grammar the reader must learn mid-scroll. Its caption, "Dotted: the day the forecast was made.", labels a *value* marker as a *day* ([Intervals.tsx](../../../web/src/components/Intervals.tsx)). One sentence under the chart also contradicts the data: "it widens because later days are harder to call". GPR's band barely widens, from 119-243 at 7 days to 111-251 at 28. Copy that the reader can see is wrong costs trust in all the other copy. Drop the sentence, or show it only when the band visibly widens. Tufte's rule to "erase redundant data-ink" applies to the whole block ([data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/chart-junk-and-data-ink-origins)).

Type, mobile layout and colour can follow rules that keep the pixel identity:

- **Type.** Datawrapper limits chart text to "two clearly different levels" ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations)). That maps onto a pixel font at two integer scales for numbers and titles, which carry the identity, and a legible face at 12 px or more, in sentence case, for the few sentences that remain.
- **Mobile.** Datawrapper numbers its annotations and moves the key below the chart on small screens ([Datawrapper, Responsive annotations](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/better-more-responsive-annotations-in-datawrapper-data-visualizations/)). Pixel markers [1] and [2] fit that. Apple's scrub-anywhere pattern replaces hover on touch ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charts)). Keyboard hints such as "[T]" and "[1]" appear only on hover or keyboard focus.
- **Colour.** The separate colour study decides the palette. This report imposes only constraints that do not depend on colour. Every pair the reader must tell apart differs by shape or weight as well as colour: real line against path (solid and heavy, thin and dotted), in range against missed (filled, hollow), band against normal line (dither, dashes). One accent marks what matters most, and everything else stays neutral.
- **The messenger.** The hero's Mercury art encodes GPR in the angle of its rays, which no reader can decode ([messenger.ts](../../../web/src/lib/messenger.ts)). It is best accepted as art. FiveThirtyEight's 2020 mascot, Fivey Fox, delivered caveats as short speech bubbles rather than paragraphs ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2020/07/this-is-how-fivethirtyeight-is-trying-to-build-the-right-amount-of-uncertainty-into-its-2020-election-data-analysis/)). The aseri sprite could carry the one "how to read this" hint the same way, as long as its lines stay factual, since a cute guide can read as softening bad news ([Real Life](https://reallifemag.com/what-the-chart-wants/)).

## Two layers cut 906 tokens to under 300 and delete nothing

Progressive disclosure has three rules that matter here. Show up front everything users need often. Avoid more than two levels, since deeper designs "typically have low usability". Label the control that reveals more with strong information scent ([NN/g, Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). Apple adds: "don't require interaction to reveal critical information" ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charts)). The Wintle result sets the boundary: about half of readers never open a tooltip. So **every number that qualifies a word stays inline, and only method, scoring detail, sources and secondary intervals move behind a tap.** Each block gets one labelled control: "How to read this", "Show the numbers", "How we score" or "Sources", never "More".

One tension needs a decision. The paragraph that says what the band is not ("Not a high and a low, nor volatility") is research-backed: text cut the misreading from 36% to almost none ([Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full)). Moving it behind a tap that half of readers never open would cost that protection. The resolution is to move its core claims onto the band itself ("8 in 10 days land here", "↑↓ 2 in 10 outside"), where nobody can miss them, and to put only the longer explanation behind "How to read this".

Caveats get three tiers instead of paragraphs. A glyph sits next to the affected number. It links to a one-line footnote in small grey type under that chart, the Datawrapper fine-print pattern. That footnote points to the "About this data" sheet for the full method. ONS adds that footnotes carry only "essential contextual information", with "no lengthy footnotes that require scrolling" ([ONS, Chart text](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/chart-text)). Our World in Data shows the source on every chart, with a "Learn more about this data" overlay behind it ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/redesigning-our-interactive-data-visualizations)).

The writing rules are simple. GOV.UK asks for sentences of 25 words or fewer, everyday words and active voice, and calls plain English "mandatory" ([GOV.UK, Clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)). ONS caps titles at 15 words. The budget can be enforced like any other contract. Add a test that renders the default view, counts its visible tokens and fails above 300. It is cheap, and it stops each new series from quietly adding back 100 tokens.

The overall shape is the "martini glass" of narrative visualisation: a tight, author-driven top that "opens up later for free exploration" ([Segel and Heer 2010](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)). Here the stem is the hero and the panel title, and the bowl is the cards, the replay and the Record.

The ranking is expert judgement built on transferred evidence, and none of the replacement strings has been shown to aseri's readers. The cited studies come from weather, health, buses and elections, not geopolitical indices. No study in the notes tests "8 in 10" against "80%" for this kind of forecast, or how lay readers understand track-record statistics. Joslyn and Savelli recommend open-ended questions asked *before* telling viewers that a graphic shows uncertainty ([Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full)), and Ripberger's review closes on "pretest with the audience" ([Ripberger et al. 2022](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/60526/noaa_60526_DS1.pdf)). Run three probes with a handful of non-expert readers, first on today's page, then on the redesign of changes 1 to 5:

- **Five-second test:** "What is this page telling you?"
- **Direction:** "Will geopolitical risk be higher or lower on Nov 23 than now?" The correct answer today is "about the same".
- **Open-ended band question:** "What does the shaded area show?", plus "Which line is the forecast?" for the path.

The changes earn their place if they move these answers.

## Conclusion

The page's numerate habits did not fail; repetition turned them into noise. Each extra "8 in 10" made the phrase less of a sentence and more of a code, and each repeated forecast pushed the one definition further down the page. So the most effective rewrite subtracts. The words that remain do more work when the page computes them daily: a title that states today's takeaway, a direction word read from the quantiles. Standing explanation does less. The protective sentences the research supports, what the band means and that 2 in 10 days land outside it, work best as labels on the marks they explain. Under the chart they are a paragraph most visitors never reach; behind a tap, about half never open them.

The paths are aseri's most unusual asset, and the conflict with the owner's design is narrower than it first looks. Few forecast sites can draw joint futures from a dependence model estimated on their own record. That makes possible what most pages cannot: animated draws, a stack of blocks that counts "8 in 10" out loud, and a replay where what happened is literally one more path. The evidence objects to only two moments: a frozen opening path that calls itself "the most likely", and a replay verdict measured against a path instead of the band. Fix those, cap the default view with a token test before the other four series go live, and the pixel-art identity becomes the way the page shows uncertainty, not decoration around it.
