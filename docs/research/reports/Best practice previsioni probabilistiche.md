# Plumbing, not the model, decides aseri's honesty

The model is a sound choice. The biggest risks to what aseri shows its users lie elsewhere: in what the chart compares, in which data vintage feeds and scores each forecast, and in whether each published score shows its uncertainty. The owner's chart complaint comes from the target and the display, not from a model bug. `paintChart` ends a gold 7-day-average line at 181 under a gold "now" marker, while the median and the bands start from the raw daily value of 165. The fix is to draw the same quantity on both sides of the origin: the daily value now, and optionally a 7-day-average forecast built from sample paths later. Shifting the forecast onto the average is not a fix. Second, GPR revises its last month by a median of about 8 to 9% (up to 57% on single days), and PortWatch publishes no vintages at all. The spike's GPR scores are therefore optimistic, and aseri must start keeping its own as-of snapshots now, because a PortWatch vintage that is not saved is lost for good. Third, 30 overlapping live forecasts cannot tell a 70% band from a nominal 80% one: even with independent hits, the test has a power of only 0.27. So every coverage and skill number needs its n, a block-bootstrap interval and a comparison with the strongest simple baseline. On aseri's own backtest those baselines tie Timestral on Suez (the 28-day mean), on Hormuz beyond two weeks (the last value) and on Brent (naive). Benchmarks favour pretrained models on aggregate, but a NeurIPS 2025 workshop study finds the opposite on competition data. The per-series baseline gate therefore matters more than any leaderboard. Fifteen of the 23 ranked items at the end are arithmetic or tests that fit the CPU-only daily GitHub Action. The rest (baseline selection, the real-time GPR rebuild, calibration fitting, an ensemble, a 7-day-average target, input experiments and test extras) are offline, optional or heavy.

## Data: GPR rewrites its last month and PortWatch keeps no history

### Keep two versions of every value: what was known, and what is scored

The forecasting literature and the forecast hubs agree on one rule that aseri does not follow yet. A forecast is generated from the data **as it was known at its origin**, and it is scored against a version of the truth **defined in advance**. Croushore and Stark built the Philadelphia Fed real-time data set as "vintages, or snapshots" for exactly this purpose ([Croushore and Stark, J. Econometrics 2001](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/1999/wp99-4.pdf?sc_lang=en)). Stark and Croushore then showed that RMSE and MAE "can look deceptively low" when computed against latest-available data ([Stark and Croushore 2002](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedpwp:01-10)). The hubverse, whose format aseri already publishes, separates the two versions explicitly. It warns that forecasting from finalized data and scoring against it "can make retrospective performance look substantially better than it really was in real time" ([hubverse target data guide](https://docs.hubverse.io/en/latest/user-guide/target-data.html)). FRED encodes the same idea as a `realtime_start` and `realtime_end` period on every value ([FRED API, real-time periods](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/docs/api/fred/realtime_period.html)).

GPR is the series where this bites hardest. Its authors state that the latest data are "preliminary and subject to revisions", update the daily file every Monday, and archive past vintages ([GPR page](https://www.matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm)). The research notes compared column `GPRD` across four archived vintages. One week later, 5 of the last 7 days had changed: 2026-10-19 fell from 248.7 to 220.8 (-11.2%). Three to four weeks later, **every one of the last 30 days had changed, with a median absolute revision of 7.6 to 9.2% and a maximum of 56 to 57%** ([GPR vintage archive](https://github.com/iacoviel/iacoviel.github.io/tree/master/gpr_archive_files)). Back-revisions also happen: in February 2024 the authors fixed missing articles from 2021 onwards ([GPR update log](https://github.com/iacoviel/iacoviel.github.io/blob/master/gpr_files/log.txt)). Revision noise of this size is comparable to the spike's GPR WQL of 0.18 to 0.23 ([spike-timestral-backtest.md](../spike-timestral-backtest.md)). The spike fed today's revised values into every origin and scored against them, as its own [limits section](../spike-timestral-backtest.md#limits-of-this-spike) admits. Stark and Croushore's result predicts that the live record will look worse than that backtest. The archive holds about 295 weekly vintages since 2022-02-24, so aseri can rebuild a true real-time GPR backtest instead of guessing the gap.

PortWatch is the opposite problem: nobody keeps its history. Its metadata documents AIS sources, a 48-hour re-count rule and UTC dates, but says nothing about update cadence, preliminary status or revisions ([PortWatch metadata](https://services9.arcgis.com/weJ1QsnbMYJlCHdG/ArcGIS/rest/services/Daily_Chokepoints_Data/FeatureServer/info/metadata)). On 2026-10-30 at 08:48 UTC, the layer's `dataLastEditDate` was 2026-10-28 and the data still ended on 2026-10-25. The 400 most recent days matched the previous day's pull exactly, including the unrevised Taiwan Strait dropout on 2026-10-23 ([layer JSON](https://services9.arcgis.com/weJ1QsnbMYJlCHdG/arcgis/rest/services/Daily_Chokepoints_Data/FeatureServer/0?f=json)). Third-party trackers claim that PortWatch revises its history; that claim is unverified. The consequence is that **aseri must keep its own PortWatch vintages starting now**, because an unsaved vintage cannot be recovered later. Wikipedia shows the third revision pattern, a change of method. In 2025 Wikimedia reclassified its March to August traffic after bots evaded detection, which lowered human pageviews by roughly 8% against the same months of 2024 ([Wikimedia Diff 2025](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/10/17/new-user-trends-on-wikipedia/)). A routine tail revision should pass silently. A rewrite that reaches further back than expected (90 days for GPR, given the 2024 fix, and 30 days for PortWatch and pageviews) should hold publication and open an issue. Revision-aware foundation models are not an alternative yet: the one 2026 proposal, VINTAGE-TS, has only a synthetic demonstration ([arXiv 2609.28576](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28576)).

The spec already appends observation deltas to the `archive` branch ([spec 6.3](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#63-daily-pipeline)), so the store mostly exists. What it lacks is the upstream freshness stamps and hashes in the manifest (HTTP `Last-Modified` for GPR, `dataLastEditDate` for PortWatch, the commit SHA for the GitHub datasets), a tested `as_of_view(series, t)` that never returns a value first seen after t, a GPR history seeded from the authors' vintages, and a scoring delay per series fixed in advance, which the evaluation section sets out.

### Every series ends on a different day, and a missing day is not a zero

Each source ends on its own day, so each series needs its own origin: its last complete day. Horizons count from that day, which [forecast-horizons.md](../forecast-horizons.md) already makes rule 1, and scores must be keyed on `(origin, h)` rather than on the publication date. The PortWatch lag is not a constant. The researchers measured 4 days on 2026-10-29 and at least 5 on 2026-10-30, and forecast-horizons.md inferred up to about 11 days just before the next weekly batch. Freshness thresholds should therefore come from a log of `dataLastEditDate`, not from a hard-coded "4 days". Treat the last PortWatch row as complete only if the layer was edited more than 24 hours after that UTC day ended. Drop the current UTC day for event-derived series. Warn at the expected lag plus 2 days and fail at plus 7. On failure, keep the previous forecast online with a "stale since" label rather than re-forecasting stale data and presenting it as fresh.

Missing values should reach the model as NaN. FPP3 notes that naive, ARIMA and dynamic regression handle gaps while ETS and STL do not ([Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, FPP3 13.9](https://otexts.com/fpp3/missing-outliers.html)). Timestral masks NaN natively and forward-fills masked positions internally with `ffill_with_mask` ([timestral repo](https://github.com/mistralai/timestral); [timestral-model.md](../timestral-model.md)), so upstream imputation adds nothing. Missing or imputed actuals must never be scored. The notes infer, without a published effect size, that forward-filled actuals favour the naive baseline. The dangerous gap is the one silently encoded as zero. [Spec 6.3](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#63-daily-pipeline) says "the alert hours treat missing days as zero". That is correct only for days up to the source's freshness cutoff, the latest event or commit timestamp. Without the cutoff, a stale file produces trailing zeros that the model reads as a ceasefire. The source also imputes values itself: alerts without an end time carry `naive=True` and a 30-minute duration ([dataset README](https://github.com/Vadimkin/ukrainian-air-raid-sirens-dataset/blob/main/datasets/README.md)).

### Brent needs a trading-day clock, and counts need count-aware scores

FPP3 re-indexes stock prices on trading days "because stock prices are not observed every day" ([FPP3 5.2](https://otexts.com/fpp3/simple-methods.html)), and [spec 6.1](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#61-the-nine-series) already forecasts Brent on trading days. The remaining danger is in scoring. A forward-filled weekend "actual" equals Friday's price, so the last-value naive scores perfectly on it, and any model looks good. Score only dates with a real print. FRED releases Brent in weekly batches (on 2026-10-28 it showed the next release as 2026-11-04) ([FRED DCOILBRENTEU](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DCOILBRENTEU)), so Brent's freshness rule must expect a weekly cadence. Because Brent is close to a random walk, naive is the benchmark that "without comparing against it, quality of more complex models cannot be meaningfully assessed" ([Hewamalage, Ackermann and Bergmeir, arXiv 2203.10716](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.10716v2)).

Count series need care in three places. Quantiles survive monotone transforms exactly: a back-transformed interval "preserves the probability coverage", but the back-transformed point is the median, not the mean ([FPP3 5.6](https://otexts.com/fpp3/ftransformations.html)). Raw-scale CRPS and WIS are dominated by large values, while scoring on a log scale approximates a probabilistic relative error, and doing so **changed the model rankings in the European COVID-19 Forecast Hub** ([Bosse et al., PLOS Comp. Biol. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10495027/); code [epiforecasts/transformation-forecast-evaluation](https://github.com/epiforecasts/transformation-forecast-evaluation)). Discrete outcomes also need a randomized PIT, because the plain PIT "is no longer uniform even when forecasts are ideal" ([scoringutils docs](https://rdrr.io/github/epiforecasts/scoringutils/man/pit_sample.html); [Czado, Gneiting and Held, Biometrics 2009](https://www.zora.uzh.ch/entities/publication/3a6b88b2-4bc3-4929-80ef-010bdd2b69ce)), and Kolassa adds that MAD, MASE and wMAPE are poorly suited to counts ([Kolassa, IJF 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v32y2016i3p788-803.html)). Hormuz at 2 to 5 transits a day is the case in point: `clip_nonnegative` produced 5,356 exact ties at zero ([public-api.md 4.6](../public-api.md)). Drone increments raise a further problem. [Spec 6.1](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#61-the-nine-series) clips the daily difference of the cumulative count at 0. A negative difference is a correction by the source, not a zero, so it should be flagged and handled as a revision. Alert-hours have a hard ceiling of 600 (25 regions times 24 hours), which allows an upper clip by the same equivariance argument.

### Clean only measurement errors, only in the context, and check the edge first

FPP3 calls replacing outliers "without thinking about why they have occurred" a "dangerous practice" ([FPP3 13.9](https://otexts.com/fpp3/missing-outliers.html)). Ledolter found that a distant outlier leaves point forecasts largely unaffected, but prediction intervals "are found to be quite sensitive" to additive outliers, and the carry-over grows near the forecast origin ([Ledolter, IJF 1989](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfor:v:5:y:1989:i:2:p:231-240)). **The last observation is therefore the most harmful place for an artifact.** aseri already has known measurement artifacts: Taiwan Strait AIS dropouts of about 25 transits between days of 240 to 400, on 2025-11-11, 2026-07-10 and 2026-10-23 ([spike-timestral-backtest.md](../spike-timestral-backtest.md)); bot surges counted as human pageviews until Wikimedia reclassified them; and the Ukrainian Air Force stopping its reports of some missile types on 2026-08-10, a break in reporting rather than in the war ([data-sources.md](../data-sources.md)). Real events are different: Hormuz falling from 78 transits a day in February 2026 to 3.2 in March is the signal, not noise.

Production practice matches this. Breck et al. found that teams investigate and fix the root cause rather than auto-patch data ([Breck et al., SysML 2019](https://mlsys.org/Conferences/2019/doc/2019/167.pdf)). The design that follows is a version-controlled anomaly register in which a `measurement_error` entry is masked to NaN in the context only, a `real_event` entry becomes a chart annotation, and a `reporting_change` entry triggers a human review of whether the pre-break history stays in the context. The scorer never reads the register. At the edge, a rule fixed in advance applies. For Taiwan, a value below 25% of the 28-day median, with the next day back above 75%, is a dropout. That rule can only be confirmed a day later, so a suspicious last value moves the origin back one day and adds a "last value under review" note. Timestral's own output cap (`quantile_real_cap_k`) is computed from the context range, so a spike left in the context also widens the allowed band ([timestral repo](https://github.com/mistralai/timestral)). Do not cut the context at a break by hand either. Hewamalage et al. warn that under breaks in the unknown future "even tsCV would grossly underestimate the generalisation error", so context length belongs in the backtest as a hyperparameter. No paper tests whether cleaning the context helps a pretrained foundation model; the evidence above is for ARIMA-type models.

### Leakage enters through three doors, and one function closes two of them

The first door is revised data used as if it were real-time. The second is any statistic computed over the full series: Hewamalage et al. name smoothing, decomposition and normalisation "over the whole series" ([arXiv 2203.10716](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.10716v2)). The third is Timestral's unknown training cutoff, since GitHub issue mistralai/timestral#3 was still unanswered on 2026-10-29 ([spike-timestral-backtest.md](../spike-timestral-backtest.md#leakage-caveat)). That third door is not hypothetical. When GIFT-Eval re-ran the original Moirai models, training-set leakage usually boosted their scores, more so at longer horizons ([GIFT-Eval, arXiv 2410.10393](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.10393)). A 2025 review found benchmark datasets inside the pretraining data of TimesFM, UniTS and TTM ([arXiv 2510.13654](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13654v3)). TS-Arena answers with live pre-registration ([TS-Arena, ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10013852)), and aseri's live record after the 2026-10-15 release is the same device.

The first two doors close with **one context builder, `make_context(store, series, as_of)`, shared by the backtest and the daily job**. This prevents what Breck et al. call feature skew between training and serving ([Breck et al. 2019](https://mlsys.org/Conferences/2019/doc/2019/167.pdf)). Every threshold, scale and detector must be computed inside that context, as of that vintage. The test is a "future poisoning" check: replace every value dated after the origin, and every vintage fetched after it, with 1e9, and assert that the forecast is bit-identical. The spike's reproducibility result (maximum absolute difference 0) shows that bit-identity is achievable.

### Validation should fire rarely and always mean something

Breck et al. describe Google's TFX data validation. A schema "codifies the expectations for correct data". Alerts must be precise, because "on-calls tend to ignore alerts that are either spammy or not actionable". Interpretable distance thresholds beat significance tests: a chi-square test between 100M normal points and the same sample with 0.01% contaminated fired at the 1% level in 7 of 10 trials ([Breck et al. 2019](https://mlsys.org/Conferences/2019/doc/2019/167.pdf)). For aseri, that means a contract per source, checked before ingest, which pandera's `DataFrameSchema` with `lazy=True` provides as a library import ([pandera docs](https://pandera.readthedocs.io/en/stable/)). The contract requires unique, monotonic daily or trading-day dates, integer counts of at least 0, alert-hours between 0 and 600, Brent above 0 and below 500, and a history that never shrinks. PortWatch adds one aseri-specific rule: errors arrive inside HTTP 200 bodies. That happened 12 times over 9 page requests in the spike, and in 2 of 6 requests on 2026-10-30. Any JSON with an `error` key or an empty `features` list is therefore a failed fetch, and it must never overwrite the store. Failures block only the affected series. The site keeps the last good forecast with a "stale since" or "data under review" label, and the job opens a GitHub issue. The notes judge pandera a better fit than Great Expectations for a single-maintainer job; that is a judgement, not a sourced finding.

## Modelling: Timestral earns its place, but simple baselines win on three of aseri's series types

### Benchmarks crown foundation models; competitions and aseri's own data are less kind

On fev-bench's 100 tasks, every recent pretrained model beats the statistical ensemble. Probabilistic skill against seasonal naive is 47.3% for Chronos-2, 42.6% for TiRex and 42.2% for TimesFM-2.5. The AutoETS, AutoARIMA, AutoTheta and AutoCES ensemble reaches 20.2%, and plain naive scores **-45.4%** ([fev-bench, arXiv 2509.26468](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26468v2)). GIFT-Eval reports that "from daily to yearly frequencies, foundation models consistently outperform other approaches". On daily data, CRPS normalised to seasonal naive is 0.392 for Moirai-Large against 0.499 for AutoARIMA ([GIFT-Eval](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10393v2)). Timestral's vendor-reported card puts it first on GIFT-Eval and fev-bench, with an SQL skill of 50.39 against 47.28 for Chronos-2 ([timestral-model.md](../timestral-model.md)). The counter-evidence is serious, though. On 15 competition datasets, "statistical models and their ensembles outperform existing foundation forecasting models by more than 8.2% in sCRPS and more than 20% in MASE". The authors blame small evaluation sets, weak baselines and train/test overlap for the inflated gains ([Olivares et al., NeurIPS 2025 workshop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19465)). A 30-dataset break-even study found zero-shot models losing to classical methods on 6 datasets ([Tan Jerome and Simon, arXiv 2607.04919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04919); code [nicolaisi/fm-breakeven](https://github.com/nicolaisi/fm-breakeven)).

None of these benchmarks contains a daily geopolitical or news series, so aseri's own backtest is the only evidence that transfers, and it shows all three ways in which simple baselines win. When the series is close to a random walk, naive wins: Brent ties it ([spec 6.2](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#62-forecasting)), as Timestral also does on stock prices, with a MAPE of 3.36% against 3.39% ([forecast-horizons.md](../forecast-horizons.md)). When the series is level plus noise, a flat mean wins: on Suez the point forecast equals the 28-day mean (1-7 bucket MASE 0.60 vs 0.60). When a recent break makes the long context misleading, the last value wins: on Hormuz it ties the model at h=30 (0.94 vs 0.94) and beats it at h=60 (1.43 vs 1.60) ([spike-timestral-backtest.md](../spike-timestral-backtest.md)). Timestral is known to adopt a new level slowly, with an error 2.43 times an oracle's 16 steps after a shift ([off-the-prior](https://github.com/Cuuper22/off-the-prior)). Aggregate wins elsewhere (median error 17 to 36% below naive on 8 of 9 series in the 121-origin replay) do not license a claim on every series. The publishing rule should therefore be a **per-series, per-bucket gate**: show the model's skill only where its WIS beats the best of naive, seasonal naive, the 28-day mean and AutoETS with a confidence interval that excludes a tie. Otherwise say plainly that a simpler method does as well.

### Average quantiles, not probabilities, if aseri ever ensembles

Combination is the most robust single upgrade in forecasting. Twelve of M4's 17 most accurate methods were combinations ([Makridakis et al., IJF 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v34y2018i4p802-808.html)). The COVID-19 Forecast Hub adopted an untrained, equal-weight ensemble because the relative skill of its components shifted over time ([Ray et al., arXiv 2201.12387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12387)). For quantile forecasts, quantile averaging (Vincentization) and probability averaging have the same mean, but the quantile average is always sharper ([Lichtendahl, Grushka-Cockayne and Winkler, Management Science 2013](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1120.1667)). A linear pool of calibrated forecasts is itself overdispersed ([Ranjan and Gneiting, JRSSB 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2009.00726.x)). With 2 to 4 members, the hub's median is either the mean or erratic. aseri should therefore take the equal-weight mean at each of the 21 levels, which keeps quantiles monotone. Because aseri's bands under-cover on GPR and over-cover on Suez, the backtest should choose between quantile averaging and the linear pool per series.

A public precedent exists. flusight-fm runs a zero-shot ensemble of Chronos-2, TimesFM 2.5, Moirai 2.0 and Timestral in a weekly GitHub Action ([CDDEP-DC/flusight-fm](https://github.com/CDDEP-DC/flusight-fm)). An ICLR 2026 paper finds portfolios of small pretrained models competitive with single large ones and cheaper than test-time fine-tuning ([Kayaalp et al., arXiv 2510.06419](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06419)). Licences narrow the pool. Chronos-2 and TimesFM-2.5 are Apache-2.0. Moirai-2.0 is CC-BY-NC-4.0, and the TimesFM 3.0 weights are "non-commercial, non-production" ([timesfm README](https://github.com/google-research/timesfm)). An ensemble is CPU-feasible (Chronos-2 has 120M parameters and TimesFM-2.5 200M), but it doubles or triples inference and the weight caches. It also creates a new model ID that restarts the live record. It belongs after the baseline gate, not before.

### Calibrate bands per series and horizon from the backtest, and budget for the restart

Time-series foundation models are better calibrated than ARIMA and N-BEATS on average, but "all autoregressive TSFMs are consistently overconfident in long-term forecasting", and "WQL alone is not necessarily a reliable indicator of calibration performance" ([Adler et al., arXiv 2510.16060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16060)). Every applied Timestral project that reports coverage finds bands slightly too narrow ([forecast-horizons.md](../forecast-horizons.md)):

| Project | Nominal band | Coverage reported |
|---|---|---|
| Stocks (Dubach) | 90% | 84.8 to 85.2% |
| Bank balance sheets | 80% | 75 to 76% |
| bdbv-fm (Ebola counts) | 90% | 84 to 90%, after widening the nowcast standard deviation by 1.3 |
| timestral-weather | 80% | 74 to 81% held out, after per-day widening factors of 1.08 to 1.28 |

aseri's 80% band held 72 to 87%, at 0.67 for GPR at h=1, and Suez over-covers at h=60 and h=90 (0.93 to 0.95).

The cheapest fix that keeps Timestral's asymmetric, heteroscedastic band shape is a conformalized quantile regression (CQR) offset. Score how far each actual falls outside [q_lo, q_hi] on a calibration set, then widen or shrink by the conformal quantile of those scores ([Romano, Patterson and Candès 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03222)). The finite-sample formula sets the data budget: n must be at least 1/alpha - 1. With 52 origins per bucket, the 80% band is easy. The 98% band (alpha = 0.02, n of at least 49) is just possible as a two-sided band and impossible tail by tail. Zero-shot models help here, because no data is spent on training and more is left for calibration ([Achour et al., arXiv 2507.08858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08858)).

In production, an online tracker can follow drift. Adaptive conformal inference bounds the long-run coverage error by (max(alpha_1, 1 - alpha_1) + gamma)/(gamma T) ([Gibbs and Candès 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170)). With one year of daily resolutions that is 5.2 points at gamma = 0.05 but 52 points at gamma = 0.005, so aseri would need a gamma of 0.02 to 0.05 and visibly jumpy bands, best started from the offline CQR offset rather than from zero. Conformal PID control generalises this to quantile tracking, and its public code takes the forecast horizon as an `ahead` parameter, which handles the h-day resolution delay ([Angelopoulos, Candès and Tibshirani 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16895); code [aangelopoulos/conformal-time-series](https://github.com/aangelopoulos/conformal-time-series)). EnbPI fits badly, because it needs a retrainable bootstrap ensemble. Isotonic level recalibration cannot widen a band beyond the model's own q0.01 to q0.99. The governance cost is decisive: [spec 6.5](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#65-covariate-scout) gives changed inference settings a new model ID, so a calibration layer restarts the live record. Decide on it before the first 30 live forecasts accumulate, or publish it as a separately labelled second model.

### A 7-day average is a different target, and its quantiles cannot be averaged

The owner's chart compares a trailing 7-day mean with a daily forecast. Building a 7-day-mean forecast from daily quantiles is not arithmetic. FPP3 notes that prediction intervals of aggregates are "more tricky due to the correlations between forecast errors" and prescribes simulation: generate sample paths, aggregate within each path, then take quantiles ([FPP3 13.5](https://otexts.com/fpp3/aggregates.html)). Summing or averaging quantiles is exact only for comonotonic variables, and it gives the widest possible spread ([Dhaene et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-6687(02)00134-8)). Timestral outputs 21 marginal quantiles and no samples, so aseri would have to add a dependence model: either a Gaussian copula across horizons, with its correlation estimated from the normal scores of backtest PITs, or the Schaake shuffle and ensemble copula coupling, which reorder samples to match the rank pattern of historical trajectories ([Schefzik, Thorarinsdottir and Gneiting 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.7149); [Schefzik 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.02079)). The derived forecast has a property worth having: the first window of the 7-day mean contains six observed days, so **the forecast starts at the current 7-day mean by construction** and its band is narrow early. No forced anchoring is needed. Modelling the smoothed series directly is simpler, but the model then ignores that six days of each early window are already fixed, and a trailing mean lags the level by about three days. Non-overlapping weekly totals suit heavy-tailed drones best, as [forecast-horizons.md](../forecast-horizons.md) already recommends, but leave only four forecast points. The weekly cycle itself should stay in the daily layer. Timestral already learns it: day-of-week covariates moved GPR by only +0.3% and +0.1% ([spike-covariates.md](../spike-covariates.md)). No study evaluates copula aggregation on foundation-model quantiles specifically; the method comes from weather post-processing.

### Never bend the forecast to meet the last value

The forecast distribution starts at h=1. fable leaves a visible gap by default (`show_gap = TRUE`). Connecting the last observation to the first forecast is a display option: "Setting this to FALSE will connect the most recent value in data with the forecasts" ([fabletools reference](https://fabletools.tidyverts.org/reference/autoplot.fbl_ts.html)). Subtracting (median at h=1 minus y_T) from every quantile would turn Timestral into an untested hybrid of naive and shape and break its calibration. A jump at the join carries information (mean reversion, a weekday effect) as long as both sides show the same quantity. On GPR, the h=1 band is already too narrow (cov80 0.67), so the jump is not evidence of error. If a level correction is ever wanted, it must be backtested as a separate model.

### Transforms and context length are experiments; fine-tuning and covariates are not worth it

Timestral's 21 quantiles are non-crossing, `clip_nonnegative=True` clamps at zero for non-negative histories, and the context can reach 32,768 steps ([timestral-model.md](../timestral-model.md)). The only crossing risk is aseri's own post-processing, such as per-level offsets. A sort fixes it, because monotone rearrangement brings the curve closer to the truth ([Chernozhukov, Fernández-Val and Galichon 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.3649)). A log1p input is exact for quantiles via `expm1`. It is untested for foundation models, which rescale every context internally, so it belongs in a per-series A/B backtest under a keep rule fixed in advance.

Context length is the same kind of question. Chronos-2 gained from extending its context to 8,192 ([Chronos-2 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15821v1)), while aseri's breaks argue for testing the last 512, 1,024 and 2,048 days against the full 15,000-day GPR history. No Timestral context ablation has been published.

Fine-tuning is not worth it. The break-even study finds that zero-shot wins below about 700 training samples when seasonality matters, and that LoRA can make short series worse ([arXiv 2607.04919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04919)). Timestral also has no fine-tuning API.

Covariates help pretrained models only when they are known over the horizon and drive the target directly. On fev-bench's 42 covariate tasks, covariate-aware models gained ([fev-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26468v2)). aseri's spike found none of 16 sets worth keeping, while a synthetic positive control cut error by 89% ([spike-covariates.md](../spike-covariates.md)). The null result is about the data, which are same-day or lagged. The daily job should stay univariate, with known-future calendars left to the scout.

## Evaluation: thirty overlapping forecasts cannot tell a 70% band from an 80% one

### WIS on 21 levels, reported per series and bucket, with its convention stated

Gneiting and Raftery set the goal as maximising "the sharpness of the predictive distributions subject to calibration". They also note that scores are "directly comparable" only on "exactly the same set of forecast situations", and that skill scores of the form (S_fcst - S_ref)/(S_opt - S_ref) "are generally improper" ([Gneiting and Raftery, JASA 2007](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)). The weighted interval score is the hub standard. On aseri's 21 symmetric levels it is exactly **WIS = (2/21) times the sum of the 21 pinball losses** ([Bracher et al., PLOS Comp. Biol. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008618); [scoringutils `wis()`](https://epiforecasts.io/scoringutils/reference/wis.html)). The researchers checked that it sits about 4% below the true CRPS for a Gaussian, because of the missing tails. Model-to-baseline ratios are unaffected, but aseri's WIS is not comparable with 23-level hub WIS ([evalkit.py](../research_notes/Best%20practice%20previsioni%20probabilistiche/code/evalkit.py)).

Two traps need stating. The first is a factor of 2: scoringrules and utilsforecast use the plain pinball loss, while FPP3, GIFT-Eval's wQL and WIS use twice it, so `scores.json` must name its convention. The second is a library bug: in scoringrules 0.11.0, `weighted_interval_score` is wrong on every backend except numba, because it adds `w_median * median` instead of `w_median * |obs - median|` ([issue #140](https://github.com/frazane/scoringrules/issues/140)). On scoringutils' documented example it returns 0.56, 12.34 and 15.94 instead of 0.36, 15.34 and 19.14, so a 10-line in-house WIS, tested against that example, is safer than the import.

Aggregation follows from the same sources. Raw WIS must never be averaged across series, because their units differ by orders of magnitude and, as Bracher et al. note, long horizons and large values "dominate average scores". Models are compared through ratios of mean scores over identical (origin, horizon) targets, never through means of per-forecast ratios, and a cross-series headline takes the geometric mean of per-series ratios, as fev-bench does. For drones, alert-hours and "World War III" pageviews, add log1p-scale WIS as a secondary view. Bosse et al. recommend log(x + 1), or a larger offset when much of the mass sits near zero ([Bosse et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10495027/)).

The spike's two metrics should leave the headline. GIFT-Eval-style WQL divides by the sum of |y|, so it explodes as that sum approaches zero, the same failure as MAPE on drones and Hormuz. MASE with a scale computed over the whole context made recent Hormuz errors look tiny (0.1 to 0.2) while level-relative WQL read 0.4 to 1.9 ([spike-timestral-backtest.md](../spike-timestral-backtest.md)). If MASE stays, its scale window should be fixed (for example the last 365 days of each origin's context) and published.

### Coverage needs its interval, and overlapping origins shrink the sample

Exact binomial arithmetic shows how little a coverage number at aseri's display threshold can say ([covsim.py](../research_notes/Best%20practice%20previsioni%20probabilistiche/code/covsim.py)):

| Resolved forecasts n | 95% CI if 80% observed | Power vs true 70% | Power vs true 60% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 0.61 to 0.92 | 0.27 | 0.71 |
| 60 | 0.68 to 0.89 | 0.44 | 0.93 |
| 120 | 0.72 to 0.87 | 0.69 | 1.00 |
| 250 | 0.74 to 0.85 | 0.96 | 1.00 |

These figures assume independent hits, and aseri's hits are not independent. In a simulation of perfectly calibrated forecasts on an AR(1) series with persistence 0.9, 30 daily origins at h=7 carried the information of about **9 independent forecasts**, and 120 origins at h=28 that of about 18. The [spec 6.4](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#64-track-record) `min_n_to_display: 30` therefore works as a display gate but is not evidence of calibration. The backtest's 72 to 87% falls inside the noise. Christoffersen's independence test rejects perfect forecasts when they overlap, because consecutive h-step hits share a future path. Run it only on h=1 hits or on origins spaced h days apart, as the weekly record dots already are.

A 21-bin PIT histogram is noise at this n: the outer bins are 0.01 wide, so 120 forecasts put 1.2 expected observations in each. A quantile-reliability plot reads better: the share of y at or below q_tau against tau for all 21 levels, with binomial bands. Fix whether a band's bounds count as inside, because integer counts sit on integer quantiles. FluSight rounded so that teams were "not penalized for missing an interval by less than one admission" ([Mathis et al., Nat. Commun. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11282251/)).

### Skill needs the strongest baseline, chosen in advance, and a confidence interval

The hubs' relative WIS is a geometric mean over a pairwise tournament. With one model and one baseline it reduces to mean(WIS_model)/mean(WIS_baseline) on common targets ([scoringutils pairwise comparisons](https://epiforecasts.io/scoringutils/reference/get_pairwise_comparisons.html)). The hubs publish it without significance tests. The European hub states it has "not used any formal statistical test for comparing forecast scores, such as the Diebold-Mariano test" ([Sherratt et al., eLife 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10238088/)). aseri should not copy that gap. For one fixed horizon, a Diebold-Mariano test on WIS differences with h - 1 Newey-West lags and the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold correction is standard ([R `forecast::dm.test`](https://pkg.robjhyndman.com/forecast/reference/dm.test.html)). statsmodels 0.15.0 implements it, but its default `lags` is max(h - 1, ceil(n^(1/3))), so pass `lags=h-1` explicitly ([statsmodels docs](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.tsa.stattools.diebold_mariano_test.html)). For horizon buckets, a stationary block bootstrap on per-origin bucket means, with the block at least the bucket's maximum horizon, is more stable ([arch bootstrap docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/timeseries-bootstraps.html)). fev-bench uses the paired bootstrap the same way ([fev-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26468v2)).

The local evidence shows why the interval matters. The spike's GPR 1-7 MASE ratio had a CI of [0.69, 0.83] over 52 weekly origins but [0.65, 1.11] over the 7 most recent ones. Detecting an improvement mu at 80% power needs about 7.8 times (sigma_LR/mu) squared effectively independent origins: 8 when the loss differential is as variable as its mean, 70 when it is three times as variable. **Expect a 30-forecast live record to give ratios whose intervals straddle 1 for most series**, and write "ties" when they do. Multiplicity adds a further trap: 9 series, 2 buckets and 3 baselines make 54 comparisons, about 3 of which "win" by chance at p < 0.05. Pre-register one primary comparison per series, against the best baseline chosen on the backtest, or use Holm's adjustment.

Two baseline upgrades follow from hub practice. First, add a **probabilistic naive baseline**: the last value plus empirical quantiles of past h-step changes. This mirrors the COVIDhub baseline ([Cramer et al., PNAS 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169655/)) and FluSight's quantile baseline ([Mathis et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11282251/)), and it stops the comparison from rewarding the model merely for having a spread. Second, report how often the model beat the baseline period by period. M6 showed that only 11 of 163 teams (6.7%) beat its simple benchmark on both tracks, and only 3 beat its forecasts in all 12 months ([Makridakis et al., arXiv 2310.13357](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13357)).

### Treat the origin as the unit of a rolling-origin backtest

The 28 cells of one origin share one future path, so bootstrapping (origin, horizon) cells independently overstates the evidence. The spike resampled whole origins but still ignored the overlap of neighbouring 90-day windows, which it calls "somewhat optimistic". A quick effective-sample estimator for published numbers is n_eff = n / (1 + 2 times the sum of the first h - 1 autocorrelations of the per-origin loss differential).

The benchmarks handle overlap in different ways. GIFT-Eval scores non-overlapping windows over the last 10% of each series and flags partial leakage in the pretraining corpora of TimesFM, Chronos and Moirai ([GIFT-Eval](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10393)). fev-bench asks contributors to declare leakage per model-task pair and replaces leaked results ([fev-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26468v2)). VIEWS gives "approximately equal weight" to test-period and true-future performance, because scores "will vary considerably over time" ([VIEWS 2023/24, arXiv 2407.11045](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11045)).

aseri's [120-day backfill](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#62-forecasting) suffers from both leaks: possible training contamination and revised GPR inputs. It should carry the label "pseudo-real-time" and a `testdata_leakage: unknown` provenance flag, and it must never be counted in the live record, which the spec already forbids.

### Score against a vintage fixed in advance, as every hub did

Every hub scored against a fixed snapshot taken after a stabilisation period. The US COVID-19 Forecast Hub scored against data "available as of November 16, 2021", excluding the last two weeks, and did not score negative observations ([Cramer et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169655/)). The European hub removed forecasts around any data point that a later release "revised ... by over 5%" ([Sherratt et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10238088/)). FluSight scored against snapshots of 2022-09-12 and 2023-06-13 ([Mathis et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11282251/)). The hubverse schema formalises this with a `versioned` target-data setting and an `as_of` column ([hubverse schema v6.0.0](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hubverse-org/schemas/main/v6.0.0/target-data-schema.json)). There are three options. First release is fast but scores against noisy values. The latest vintage restates the public record retroactively. A **fixed stabilisation lag L per series** is the hub practice. aseri should score each target once, against the vintage at `target_end_date + L`, freeze the score, and store `observation_as_of` with it. Reasonable starting values are L = 35 days for GPR (revisions above 1% appear 3 to 4 weeks back), about 14 for PortWatch until its revisions are measured, and 7 for the rest. Measure L later from aseri's own archive, as the age after which 95% of revisions fall below 1% of the value, and publish the latest-vintage score for GPR as a sensitivity check only.

### What hubs and competitions publish

| Venue | Probabilistic metric | Baseline | Uncertainty on scores |
|---|---|---|---|
| US COVID-19 Hub ([Cramer et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169655/)) | WIS on 23 quantiles, 50% and 95% coverage | Last value, spread from past changes | None; relative WIS, ranks |
| European COVID-19 Hub ([Sherratt et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10238088/)) | WIS, pairwise relative WIS, coverage | Last value, expanding uncertainty | IQRs only |

What not to copy is the hubs' rankings without intervals and GIFT-Eval's division by the sum of |y| on near-zero series. Coverage in the hubs was "typically below nominal": the COVID ensemble alone reached 0.53 and 0.90 on its 50% and 95% bands ([Cramer et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169655/)), and FluSight's 95% coverage fell with horizon, from 85.8% to 77.9% in 2022-23. aseri's slightly narrow bands are normal for the field. Hiding them is not.

## Visualization: the forecast must continue the quantity the history draws

### Why the live chart shows two "nows"

The repo's code explains the complaint exactly. In [`web/src/lib/chart.ts`](../../../web/src/lib/chart.ts), `paintChart` sets `last = values[origin]` and starts both the median polyline and every band from it. It then draws the 7-day average as a solid gold polyline over the daily dots, and marks `averages[origin]` with a 3x3 gold square. The origin column therefore holds two different "now" points, 181 and 165, and the brighter, marked one is not where the forecast starts. The daily values are 1 to 2 pixel dots in label grey, so the eye reads the gold line as "the series". The median that continues it is pale gold, at a contrast of only **1.26:1 against the gold line**, so it looks like the same line dropping 16 points and then sawing through the weekend. The existing test asserts that `forecastAt` starts from the last daily value. That is true, and it is exactly why the test missed the bug.

Readers forecasting from graphs anchor on the last data point and under-adjust ([Harvey and Reimers 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/13591)). The format of the history changes the forecasts lay people draw from it ([Reimers and Harvey, IJF 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/29623/)). Weekend reporting dips "can seem like a sign that trends are improving" ([COVID Tracking Project](https://covidtracking.com/analysis-updates/how-day-of-week-effects-impact-covid-19-data)). aseri's weekend forecast dip to about 104 from a displayed 181 builds that misreading into the page. [Spec 6.8](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#68-website-structure-and-behaviour) adds to it by making the GPR 7-day average the hero's giant number, next to a single-day forecast sentence.

### What established forecast displays do at the join

No controlled study compares "joined to the last observation" with "starting at h=1" for lay readers. Every strong practice shares one invariant: history and forecast are the same quantity. The Bank of England's fan chart plots twelve-month inflation on both sides, and was designed "to focus attention on the whole of the forecast distribution, rather than on small changes to the central projection" ([Britton, Fisher and Whitley, BoE Quarterly Bulletin 1998](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/1998/q1/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart)). hubVis plots model output against `target_data` of the same target ([hubVis reference](https://hubverse-org.github.io/hubVis/reference/plot_step_ahead_model_output.html)). The European hub turns daily source data into "the count of new hospitalisations ... per week" before forecasting, rather than mixing a smoothed history with a raw forecast ([European hub targets](https://www.github.com/european-modelling-hubs/covid19-forecast-hub-europe/wiki/targets-and-horizons)). No source found extends a smoothed history line with a raw-value forecast.

That leaves two consistent designs. **Design A puts the daily value on both sides** and fits [spec 6.2](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#62-forecasting) as written: daily values become the dominant history mark, as connected dots or a thin line at least as bright as the median, the "now" marker sits on `values[origin]`, and the 7-day line turns thinner, dimmer or dashed, stops at the origin and carries a "7-day avg" label, with an optional tint on weekend columns. **Design B puts the average on both sides**: the 7-day-average forecast comes from sample paths, as the modelling section describes, which needs a spec change and a new Hubverse target. Design A should ship now, and design B only if users need the smoothed view. In both designs, every number shown next to another must be the same quantity: the hero's giant number says "7-day average" on its face, and cards label their "In h days" range as a single day.

Two drawing details matter. First, `forecastAt` interpolates linearly from the last value to day 1, which pinches every band to zero width at the origin and draws values that no forecast states. fable and hubVis draw only at forecast dates, so per-day band columns starting at h=1 are more truthful. Second, a pinched band claims the last value is exact. That is defensible for PortWatch counts but not for GPR, which revises. Since 2007 the BoE GDP fan has extended backwards as a "backcast" for exactly this reason ([BoE fan charts 2017](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/inflation-report/2017/fan-charts-aug-2017.pdf)).

### Show uncertainty as frequencies and demote the median

Showing uncertainty helps lay decisions and costs little trust. Probabilistic weather forecasts improved precautionary decisions and trust compared with deterministic ones ([Joslyn and LeClerc, J. Exp. Psych.: Applied 2012](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/joslyn:2012:UFID/index.html)). In five UK surveys (n = 5,780), a numeric range barely reduced trust, while an unquantified verbal caveat cost more ([van der Bles et al., PNAS 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1913678117); [summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200323152106.htm)).

Among formats, frequency displays lead. In an incentivized task, quantile dotplots reached about 97% of the optimal payoff, 5 points above control ([Fernandes et al., CHI 2018](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2018-UncertainBusDecisions-CHI.pdf)), and they reduced the variance of probability estimates ([Kay et al., CHI 2016](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/when-ish-is-my-bus)). Adding a mean to an uncertainty display biases judgments toward discounting the uncertainty ([Kale, Kay and Hullman, TVCG 2021](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/2020-vis-effect-size-judgements)). Hard error-bar edges create a binary inside/outside reading that gradient encodings avoid ([Correll and Gleicher, TVCG 2014](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf)). Spiegelhalter et al. advise frequencies with "a clearly defined denominator of constant size" and "a less-is-more approach", and note that the BoE "has always refused to include a numerical central estimate when first releasing the graphic" ([Spiegelhalter, Pearson and Short, Science 2011](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/spiegelhalter_visualizing.pdf)).

aseri's "8 in 10" and "5 in 10" wording matches this evidence. Its pale-gold median, the brightest mark in the forecast zone at 16.3:1, does not. Demote the median below the band edges and the daily marks, and list the range before "most likely". Hypothetical outcome plots and spaghetti lines need joint sample paths, so with marginal quantiles alone any drawn trajectory would be invented. A 20-dot quantile dotplot for a selected day is the best-supported optional addition. The 2024 Bernanke review asked the BoE to drop its fan charts in favour of publishing its forecast-error record ([BoE review](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/independent-evaluation-office/forecasting-for-monetary-policy-making-and-communication-at-the-bank-of-england-a-review/forecasting-for-monetary-policy-making-and-communication-at-the-bank-of-england-a-review)). Its critique targets judgment-built fans, but its remedy, the error record, is one aseri already plans.

### Text, not legends, prevents the known misreadings

In Savelli and Joslyn's experiments, 36% of participants read a predictive-interval graphic as daytime highs and nighttime lows. With the same interval in text, 94% identified the numbers correctly. A prominent key, blurred lines and a connecting line did not prevent the error ([Joslyn and Savelli, Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full)). Novices also read the hurricane cone's widening as a storm growing in size ([Padilla, Ruginski and Creem-Regehr 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5626802/)). The NHC states its coverage plainly: the cone holds "two-thirds of historical official forecast errors" ([NHC](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/aboutcone.shtml?text)).

aseri has four analogous risks: band edges read as the series' highs and lows or as the weekday swing, a widening fan read as rising volatility, the weekend sawtooth read as a collapse, and the day-28 median read as a target. The cited mitigation is text beside the chart, which the readout and intervals block already provide. That text should say what the band is ("where the daily value should land 8 days in 10"), what it is not (not a high or low, not volatility: it widens because later days are harder to call), give the complement ("about 2 days in 10 land outside") and explain the weekday cycle ("compare Saturday with Saturday"). This wording is built from the sources' advice and is not itself tested.

### A track record that cannot flatter itself

A claimed AI forecasting gain over the CDC COVID-19 ensemble for 2024/25 turned out to be "attributable to information leakage in the retrospective forecasting setup", because data revisions were ignored ([Bracher and Funk, arXiv 2608.05883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05883), a preprint). FiveThirtyEight publishes calibration plots for every forecast since 2008 and says when results were not well calibrated ([FiveThirtyEight, Checking our work](https://projects.fivethirtyeight.com/checking-our-work)).

[Spec 6.4](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#64-track-record) already gets the core right: live and retrospective records are separate, the headline is live only, a minimum n applies, and the dot rule is fixed. Three additions close the remaining gaps. The replay must state which vintage its white "real" dots are ("data as of ..."), or it becomes a mild hindsight display for GPR. "Backtest" must appear where each retrospective forecast is drawn, in the slider track and the panel title, not only in a footnote. Coverage should read "expected about 8 in 10; got 23 of 30" with its noise: at n = 30 the standard error is 7.3 points, so anything from about 66% to 94% is consistent with an honest band. A one-line legend saying "about 2 in 10 dots should be red if the range is honest" keeps 0 of 8 from reading as a triumph and 3 of 8 from reading as a failure.

### Contrast and colour rules the current tokens break

WCAG requires 3:1 contrast for "each line in a graph" unless the same information appears in text ([W3C, SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast)), and forbids colour as the only distinguishing cue ([W3C, SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). Measured against the background #071324, the [spec 6.9](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#69-design-system) tokens score:

| Token | Contrast with the background |
|---|---|
| Gold | 12.9:1 |
| Pale gold | 16.3:1 |
| Muted gold | 4.6:1 per pixel |
| Label grey | 8.0:1 |
| Frame | 1.6:1 |

Three pairs fail as distinctions. The median and the 7-day line differ at only 1.26:1, which is the same defect that makes them read as one line. Past and replayed dots differ at 2.3:1. The frame passes only because the forecast zone is also named in text. Each pair needs a second cue (dash pattern, shape or text), and the canvas's `aria-label` should name the quantity, the anchor value and date, and the ranges shown.

## Testing: an injected plot function turns the chart bug into a ten-line test

### What the ML testing literature asks of a frozen pretrained model

Breck et al.'s ML Test Score has 28 tests in four sections. The final score is the minimum section score, "because we believe all four sections are important" ([Breck et al., IEEE Big Data 2017](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/aad9f93b86b7addfea4c419b9100c6cdd26cacea.pdf)). For a frozen model, the training items drop out and six carry the weight: a schema for every input (Data 1), "regularly testing against a very simple baseline model" (Model 5), an automated end-to-end test with "a simpler model" for speed (Infrastructure 3), a gate that can "bless the model or veto it" against the previous version with tight thresholds (Infrastructure 4), watching "the initial occurrence of any NaNs or infinities" (Monitor 5), and prediction-quality monitoring as soon as labels arrive (Monitor 7). The notes' reading of the rubric puts aseri at about 1 in Monitoring today, which caps its overall score however strong the other sections are. Amershi et al. add that "a human remains in the loop" to spot-check errors ([Amershi et al., ICSE-SEIP 2019](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2019/03/amershi-icse-2019_Software_Engineering_for_Machine_Learning.pdf)). The repo matches this picture: `pipeline/aseri/` has no scoring module, no schemas and no clamp for negative count quantiles yet. [`forecast.py`](../../../pipeline/aseri/forecast.py) only sorts.

### Five layers, from seconds to every run

**Layer 1: unit and property tests, on every push.** Hypothesis property tests check post-processing, scoring and dates. Published quantiles must be ordered, finite and non-negative for counts for any stub output, and a NaN must raise. A Hubverse-style check confirms that `target_end_date` equals `reference_date + horizon`, which hubValidations does not test by default ([hubValidations](https://hubverse-org.github.io/hubValidations/articles/validate-submission.html)). Hypothesis derandomizes automatically when `CI` is set, so these tests cannot flake on GitHub Actions ([Hypothesis profiles](https://hypothesis.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/api.html#built-in-profiles)). WIS gets three independent checks: the scoringutils worked example (0.36, 15.34, 19.14), the identity between the interval form and the pinball form (which would have caught the scoringrules bug), and algebraic properties such as a point forecast scoring |y - m|, translation invariance, scale equivariance and naive scoring exactly 1 against itself. The chart gets geometry tests through the existing seam: `paintChart(plot, ...)` takes an injected `plot` function, so a spy can record every pixel without a canvas. The test that fails on today's code is a sketch with illustrative helper names:

```ts
test("the 'now' marker and the forecast start on the same daily value", () => {
  const px = new Map<string, string>()                 // "x,y" -> last colour plotted
  const plot = (x: number, y: number, c: string) => px.set(`${x},${y}`, c)
  const fc = fixtureWithOrigin({ value: 165, average: 181 })

    expect(px.get(`${xo + dx},${yNow + dy}`)).toBe(colors.now)  // marker on the daily value
```

Companion tests assert that the readout text for each day equals `values[d]` or the median at that step, that band order holds in every column (80% top, then 50% top, median, 50% bottom, 80% bottom) under fast-check over random ordered quantiles ([fast-check](https://fast-check.dev/docs/core-blocks/runners/)), and that WCAG ratios computed from the tokens pass.

**Layer 2: integration, on every push, with recorded fixtures and the stub model.** A recording stub proves that each context ends at its own reference date, and that a second run with more data, including a GPR revision, leaves earlier files byte-identical. This is GluonTS's "using only the data before them" rule ([GluonTS evaluation API](https://ts.gluon.ai/stable/api/gluonts/gluonts.evaluation.html)). Every published JSON validates against Draft 2020-12 schemas. python-jsonschema checks formats only when a `format_checker` is passed ([python-jsonschema](https://python-jsonschema.readthedocs.io/en/stable/validate/)). The web fixtures validate against the same schemas, so web tests cannot drift from what the pipeline publishes. A cross-file contract closes the data-side half of the chart bug: `series.json`'s last observation, the last date in `actuals.json` and `latest.json`'s `reference_date` and last value must agree. Code-level metamorphic tests cover the edges: units multiplied by 1,000, shuffled rows rejected, and a constant series with no division by zero. In the one published case study on a deep forecaster, such relations found 8 previously unknown issues and caught 29 of 44 valid mutants ([Dwarakanath et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06632)).

**Layer 3: the real model, opt-in, weekly and on pin changes.** A golden-output test compares quantiles on a frozen fixture with an rtol of 1e-4. A provenance test re-forecasts one archived live date from its archived vintage and must reproduce the published quantiles. Model-level metamorphic checks (scale and shift equivariance, constant series, seasonality kept) run with tolerances set from the first runs, and their failures are documented as model properties.

**Layer 4: a pre-publish gate in `daily.yml`, on every run.** The gate applies Breck's Infrastructure 4 to real outputs. Quantiles that are not finite, ordered and non-negative for counts fail the series. A median outside history-derived bounds, or a band width of zero or absurd against recent daily moves, fails beyond a loose bound and warns beyond a tight one, with both bounds calibrated on the 120-day backfill. A large change from yesterday's forecast on shared target dates only warns, and a schema failure fails the job.

**Layer 5: live monitoring in the score stage.** It treats coverage like a VaR backtest. For each series, bucket and level, it counts misses on one origin per week and classifies them into Basel traffic-light zones from the cumulative binomial: green below 95%, yellow up to 99.99%, red beyond. For N = 250 at p = 0.01, that gives green for 0 to 4 misses, yellow for 5 to 9 and red for 10 or more ([MathWorks, VaR backtesting](https://www.mathworks.com/help/risk/overview-of-var-backtesting.html)). The check runs in both directions, because over-wide bands are miscalibration too. A red zone opens a GitHub issue and never fails the job.

### Thresholds that cannot flake

Three rules keep the suite honest. Anything random in a test uses a fixed seed. Anything that depends on the real model or on live data is a warn/fail gate or a `slow` test, never a default pytest assertion. Any tolerance on a rate is a binomial acceptance region sized to the actual n and widened for autocorrelation. That last rule exposes a mismatch in the spec. For nominal 80%, the 99% acceptance region is [0.60, 0.97] at n = 30 and [0.70, 0.89] at n = 120. The covariate scout's fixed 0.70 to 0.90 coverage window ([spec 6.5](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#65-covariate-scout)) is therefore a sensible 99% test only at roughly 60 to 120 independent hits, and too strict at n = 30.

## Conclusion

The new finding is that, for a site like aseri, honesty is mostly a set of cheap contracts rather than a property of the model: one quantity on both sides of the origin, one context builder for live and backtest, one scoring vintage fixed in advance, and one interval attached to every score. Each contract can be enforced by a short deterministic test, and the chart bug got through because no such test existed. Two of the contracts are time-critical. PortWatch vintages that are not snapshotted are gone, and the scoring vintage, the primary baseline and any calibration layer must be settled before the live record passes 30 forecasts, because changing them later means restating or restarting it.

Expect the live record to look worse than the backtest. Revisions, possible training contamination and overlapping origins all flatter the backtest, and an honest page should prepare its readers for that rather than explain it away afterwards. The open unknowns that only aseri's own data can settle are how PortWatch revises, Timestral's training cutoff, whether log1p inputs or shorter contexts help, and how lay readers interpret coverage counts. Until the live record answers them, "ties the naive method" is a respectable result to publish.
