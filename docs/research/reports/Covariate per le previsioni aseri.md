# Only known-future covariates sharpen aseri's forecasts

The owner's impression does not hold for aseri. A covariate improves a foundation model's out-of-sample forecast only when it carries information about the target's future that the target's own history lacks, and only when that information exists on the forecast day. A significant in-sample coefficient or correlation shows neither. On fev-bench, the one benchmark with a clean covariate ablation, covariates cut the error of Chronos-2 and TabPFN-TS by **10 to 13%** ([fev-bench, §7.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.26468)). That gain comes almost entirely from tasks whose covariates are **known over the forecast horizon**, such as temperature for power load or promotions for retail, where the net gain is 14 to 20%. On tasks with **past-only** covariates the net effect is +3 to +4%, a slight loss. The gain also shrinks as covariates are added, from -26% with one covariate to -7% with eight or more ([computed from the fev-bench results](https://github.com/autogluon/fev/tree/main/benchmarks/fev_bench/results)). Macro forecasting, conflict forecasting and Google Flu Trends teach the same lesson: compressed or targeted information beats stacked raw predictors, and the target's own history is the hardest signal to beat. aseri's candidates (Brent, GPR sub-indexes, pageviews, other chokepoints, drone counts) are past-only, move on the same day as the targets and are published with lags. That is why the October 2026 spike kept none of 16 covariate sets ([spike-covariates.md](../spike-covariates.md)). Three narrow families are still worth testing. The first is deterministic calendars: PortWatch history already holds 8 Lunar New Years, not one. The second is archived weather forecasts, which cover the whole 7-day horizon of the air-raid and drone series. The third is "gap-filling" rows, which feed fresher data into the days a lagging source has not yet published. A rigorous test needs weekly origins, a sealed holdout, autocorrelation-robust tests on paired WIS, a liberal screen followed by a strict confirmation, and a minimum detectable effect of 5%. Overlapping 28-day windows leave only about 17 independent comparisons a year, and the iid bootstrap that the spike and spec 6.5 use rejects a true null 16 to 42% of the time.

## Covariates cut benchmark error by 10 to 20%, but only when their future is known

fev-bench is the only general benchmark built to measure covariates. It has 100 tasks: 30 with known dynamic covariates, 24 with past dynamic covariates and 19 with static ones. The older collections it draws from (Monash, GIFT-Eval, BOOM) "lack covariates", so GIFT-Eval offers no covariate result at all ([fev-bench v4, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.26468)). On the 42 tasks with dynamic covariates, the authors ran two models with and without them. Chronos-2 scored 47.0% skill with covariates against 40.9% without, and TabPFN-TS 42.5% against 34.1%, and the authors call covariates "a key driver of their strong results" ([fev-bench v4, §7.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.26468)). As error ratios, that is **-10.3% scaled quantile loss for Chronos-2 and -12.7% for TabPFN-TS**. Modelling several targets jointly, with no covariates, is worth far less: about -1.3% on 35 multivariate tasks (same source). That number matters for aseri. Forecasting a past-only covariate and feeding the forecast back in adds nothing beyond the joint history, which is exactly what a multivariate model already does.

Splitting fev-bench's public per-task results by covariate type shows where the gain lives. The research notes computed paired geometric-mean error ratios of a covariate-using model over a univariate one, with 90% bootstrap intervals over tasks and leaked tasks excluded ([fev_covariate_split.py](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/code/fev_covariate_split.py); [results CSVs](https://github.com/autogluon/fev/tree/main/benchmarks/fev_bench/results); [tasks.yaml](https://github.com/autogluon/fev/blob/main/benchmarks/fev_bench/tasks.yaml)).

| Covariate model / univariate model | No-covariate tasks (58) | Known-future tasks (30) | Past-only tasks (12) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronos-2 / TiRex | -4.0% [-6.1, -2.1] | **-17.6% [-22.9, -12.3]** | -1.2% [-5.1, +2.5] |
| Timestral-209M / TiRex | -8.9% [-12.0, -6.1] | **-24.5% [-31.3, -17.7]** | -5.3% [-8.9, -1.4] |
| TabPFN-TS (known only) / TiRex | +8.2% [+5.0, +11.7] | **-12.9% [-19.9, -6.1]** | +6.6% [+1.5, +12.2] |

Net of each model's edge on no-covariate tasks, **known-future covariates are worth about -14% (Chronos-2), -17% (Timestral) and -20% (TabPFN-TS)**, while past-only covariates are worth +3% and +4%: nothing. TabPFN-TS works as a placebo here. It ignores past covariates, and its ratio on past-only tasks (+6.6%) matches its no-covariate ratio (+8.2%), so the past-only subset is not intrinsically easier or harder. The cross-model split confounds task mix with model quality, so the within-model -10 to -13% is the cleaner headline.

The per-task numbers show what kind of covariate wins. Air temperature for hourly energy load gives Chronos-2/TiRex ratios of 0.55 to 0.71. Published load and renewable forecasts for German day-ahead prices give 0.48, but only 0.90 to 0.95 for France, Belgium and PJM. Store openings and promotions at Rossmann give 0.53 at a 48-day horizon, and holidays at Favorita give 0.66 at 28 days. Each is a near-deterministic driver whose future value is known or forecast far better than the target. Eight known event-calendar dummies on M5 give 1.00 to 1.01, and the past-only macro, COVID and wind-farm tasks sit at 0.96 to 1.10 ([results CSVs](https://github.com/autogluon/fev/tree/main/benchmarks/fev_bench/results)). One caveat inflates the headline. The GEFCom-derived energy tasks list `airtemperature` as known, and GEFCom distributed observed station temperatures. So part of the largest gain is probably oracle information that would shrink with real weather forecasts. This is an inference: fev-bench does not discuss oracle against forecast covariates.

Studies outside fev-bench agree on direction and add warnings. On German day-ahead prices, the covariate-capable TabPFN-3 (CRPS 9.15) and Chronos-2 (11.80) beat the covariate-free Moirai-2 (16.20). The same paper restates that "gains from including exogenous variables are limited" for models "designed primarily for univariate forecasting" ([Lipiecki and Weron 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00089)). In Singapore and Australian electricity demand with about 30 weather, price and calendar features, Chronos-2 gained at daily horizons (most at 365 days) but lost slightly at every hourly horizon. Moirai became 22 to 46% worse at daily horizons of 1 to 60 days, and ChronosX's one-hour error rose from 0.63 to 8.81 MAPE ([arXiv 2602.05390](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05390); values as extracted from its tables). When covariates are noisy, misaligned or missing, exogenous-fusion models end up 32 to 62% worse than an endogenous-only floor ([arXiv 2607.05452](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05452)). The research found no benchmark with geopolitical, conflict or shipping-traffic covariate tasks. aseri's own spike is the only direct evidence for its domain.

## Stacking significant covariates erodes the gain instead of compounding it

"More covariates, more accuracy" fails first on fev-bench itself. The Chronos-2/TiRex advantage is **-25.8% with one dynamic covariate, -14.3% with two or three, -11.6% with four to seven, and -6.8% with eight or more**; Timestral runs -37.2%, -19.3%, -19.2% and -10.3% ([results CSVs](https://github.com/autogluon/fev/tree/main/benchmarks/fev_bench/results)). The one-covariate tasks are the strong-driver energy tasks, so the split is cross-task and confounded. But no slice shows gains compounding with count. Nine past turbine sensors in the KDD Cup wind task give 0.96 to 1.02, and the 30-feature demand study above shows features turning a foundation model worse. TabPFN-TS withholds past covariates altogether because they "consistently degraded its accuracy" ([fev-bench v2, Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26468v2)).

Macroeconomic forecasting settled this question two decades ago. Stock and Watson compressed 215 predictors into a few principal-component "diffusion indexes", which beat autoregressions, small VARs and leading-indicator models. The gain came from compression, not from adding raw regressors ([Stock and Watson 2002](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bes:jnlbes:v:20:y:2002:i:2:p:147-62)). Ten years later, pretesting, Bayesian model averaging, empirical Bayes and bagging over many regressors were "inferior to the DFM forecasts" for most of 143 series ([Stock and Watson 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jnlbes:v:30:y:2012:i:4:p:481-493)). Boivin and Ng found that **factors from as few as 40 pre-screened series often match or beat factors from all 147**, because extra series bring cross-correlated noise ([NBER w9829](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9829/w9829.pdf)). Bai and Ng's targeted predictors improved forecasts at every horizon, but the selected set changed with the horizon and the sample period ([Bai and Ng 2008](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:146:y:2008:i:2:p:304-317)). The forecast-combination puzzle points the same way. Estimated "optimal" weights lose to simple averages because estimation error dominates when the candidates are many relative to the sample ([Elliott, combination survey](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~gelliott/Combination.pdf)). Picking covariates by in-sample fit is the same estimation problem.

Google Flu Trends is the cautionary tale for exactly the owner's heuristic. Its builders searched **50 million search terms to fit 1,152 data points**. From August 2011 the model then "missed high for 100 out of 108 weeks", and a model on three-week-old official CDC data beat it out of sample (MAE 0.311 against 0.486) ([Lazer et al. 2014, Science](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-0e0d-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)). Each term it kept was "significant" in sample. Together they overfit.

The deeper reason is that significance and forecast gain answer different questions. The strongest case for the owner's intuition comes from Inoue and Kilian. They argue that the in-sample versus out-of-sample gap comes mostly from power, and that in-sample tests are "typically more credible" about **population** predictability ([CEPR DP3671](https://cepr.org/publications/dp3671)). A significant link is real information. But aseri's question is narrower. Does this forecasting method, fed this covariate at its real publication lag, score better on the next 28 days? That is the finite-sample question Giacomini and White formalised, and it holds for "any well-behaved loss function", including WIS ([McCracken 2020](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2020/2020-050.pdf)). Diebold puts it bluntly: the DM test "is a test for comparing forecasts, not models" ([NBER w18391](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18391)). aseri's own data show the gap. Bab-el-Mandeb transits lead Suez physically, with a detrended correlation of 0.18 at a 5 to 6 day lag, which explains about 3% of the variance. Fed to Timestral, they changed Suez WQL by only -0.1 to -0.4%, not significantly. "World War III" pageviews co-move with GPR on the same day, yet they made GPR's 8-30 day bucket worse. The failure is not the model's. In a synthetic control with a clean 5-day lead, Timestral-209M cut its error by 89% ([spike-covariates.md](../spike-covariates.md)). Sets were not additive either. Brent, GPR Threats and Bab-el-Mandeb together made Hormuz worse on days 31-90 than Brent alone (+2.4% against +1.7%) (same source).

## Conflict forecasters found a series' own past the hardest predictor to beat

aseri's targets are daily measures of incidence and intensity, the regime where conflict forecasting has the most evidence. In the 2023/24 VIEWS prediction challenge, the benchmark `conflictology_country12` "uses the set of values from each of the past 12 months for the same country as the forecast distribution" ([VIEWS challenge, arXiv 2407.11045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11045)). As of February 2025, **that benchmark led the rankings at both country and sub-national level**, ahead of models with around 100 features ([PRIO](https://www.prio.org/news/3602)). VIEWS reports that derived conflict history is "the most important driver of our forecasts" ([CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101069312/reporting/it)). The univariate foundation model aseri runs is the daily analogue of that benchmark: it replays and extrapolates the series' own dynamics.

Structural covariates mostly predict where conflict happens, not when. Mueller and Rauh showed that published covariate models lose about 20 points of AUC within countries. Rainfall falls from 0.85 to 0.58 for civil war, and economic shocks and institutions from 0.87 to 0.57. Newspaper topics hold up far better, falling only from 0.87 to 0.82 ([Mueller and Rauh, working paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f88439/f88439.pdf); [APSR 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4EABB473AFE18F157EEDE4339F34ABB0/S0003055417000570a.pdf/reading_between_the_lines_prediction_of_political_violence_using_newspaper_text.pdf)). Those are annual onset results. No evidence was found that text signals help daily intensity series 7 to 28 days ahead.

Machine-coded event data, the obvious source of "fresh" covariates, is unreliable at daily resolution. GDELT correlates with hand-coded protest data at only 0.222, and **only 21% of GDELT's valid URLs referred to real protests** after filtering ([Wang et al., Science 2016](https://people.cs.vt.edu/~naren/papers/1502.full.pdf)). Correlations improve at weekly or monthly aggregation and during large upticks (same source). Search data are nowcasting tools, "predicting the present" rather than forecasting ahead ([Choi and Varian](https://dataedge.berkeley.edu/2012/schedule/predicting-present-google-trends.html)). Prediction markets are forward-looking. But political prices are "compressed toward 50%" across 353 million trades ([arXiv 2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.19520)), and Polymarket's terms forbid redistributing or commercially exploiting its data ([data-sources.md](../data-sources.md)). For aseri, then, the strongest predictor is the one it already uses, and the covariate families with the best conflict-forecasting record work at horizons and resolutions aseri does not publish.

## aseri's candidates mostly fail the lead-time test, with three exceptions

The textbook rule decides most of aseri's candidates before any test runs. A regression forecast "requires forecasts of the predictors", except for calendar predictors, which are "all known in advance". The fallback is predictors lagged by at least the horizon ([FPP3 §7.6](https://otexts.com/fpp3/forecasting-regression.html)). Foundation models encode the same rule. Chronos-2 sets past-only covariates to missing over the horizon ([Chronos-2 report, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15821v1)). TabPFN-TS drops them ([README](https://github.com/PriorLabs/tabpfn-time-series)). TimesFM-2.5's XReg requires dynamic covariates to span context plus horizon ([source](https://github.com/google-research/timesfm/blob/master/src/timesfm/timesfm_2p5/timesfm_2p5_base.py)). Timestral treats covariates as conditioning rows that are never forecast ([spike-covariates.md](../spike-covariates.md)). A past-only covariate can therefore help only if it leads the target by at least the forecast step after its publication lag. The spike's worst-case lags were 4 days for PortWatch, 7 for GPR as a covariate, 8 for Brent from FRED, 1 for Wikipedia and alerts, and 0 for the drones file. Once those are masked, nothing aseri publishes leads another series by 7 to 28 days.

Spec 6.1 also sets different horizons per series ([spec 6.1](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#61-the-nine-series)). The horizon is **28 days for GPR, Suez and Taiwan, 14 for "World War III" views, Brent, Hormuz and Bab-el-Mandeb, and 7 for air-raid alerts and drones**, so a "28 days ahead" framing overstates the task for six series. That shapes which covariates can matter.

**Deterministic calendars** are the first exception, and the cheapest. Lunar New Year cut Taiwan Strait's median holiday-week error by 27% in the spike. Diluted over a year of origins it gave -1.7% WQL, on one event ([spike-covariates.md](../spike-covariates.md)). The single event is an artefact of the spike's 52-origin window, not of the data. PortWatch starts in 2019, and the spike's own rationale table found the holiday week at 0.84 to 0.97 of the prior three weeks **in 6 of 8 years**. Natural extensions are the other Chinese and Taiwanese holidays, and Lunar New Year and Golden Week shifted by the Asia-Europe sailing time for the Red Sea pair. Both are hypotheses with a mechanism ([catalogue](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/catalogue.md)). Hand-built political calendars belong here too, as long as each entry carries its announcement date: summits, OPEC+ meetings, US-Iran talks, Taiwan's 20 May and 10 October. Large PLA drills followed exactly such dates. Joint Sword-2024A came three days after the inauguration, and Joint Sword-2024B followed the National Day speech ([ASPI](https://www.pressurepoints.aspi.org.au/taiwan-and-the-taiwan-strait/chinas-military-coercion/chinas-large-scale-exercises-since-2022); [Global Taiwan](https://globaltaiwan.org/2024/10/the-joint-sword-2024b-exercise/)).

**Archived weather forecasts** are the second exception, and the only free source of honest non-calendar known-future values. Open-Meteo's Previous Runs API returns the value forecast N×24 hours earlier, for N of 1 to 7, with most models archived from January 2024 ([Open-Meteo Previous Runs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/previous-runs-api)). Its Historical Forecast API stitches together each run's first hours, so it is close to observed weather and would leak in a backtest ([Historical Forecast API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api)). A 7-day lead is a weak horizon for 28-day series, but it covers **the entire published horizon of alerts and drones**. The mechanism is stated, though the evidence is qualitative and contradictory. Russian planners check the weather on the route and at the target before jet Shahed launches ([Censor.net](https://censor.net/en/news/4024498/air-force-1-900-jet-powered-shaheds-launched)), and air-defence effectiveness fell "20-30%" in overcast weather. Yet "Shaheds fly in any weather" ([Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/ukraines-autumn-rains-help-shahed-type-drones-evade-ukrainian-air-defense/)). For the Taiwan Strait, typhoons closed ports during Saola and Bavi, and Typhoon Dolphin brought traffic controls on northbound ships ([MarineLink](https://marinelink.com/news/disrupts-shipping-typhoon346698); [Cyprus Shipping News](https://cyprusshippingnews.com/2026/07/14/typhoon-bavi-hits-taiwan-china-disrupting-pacific-vessel-supply/); [Baird Maritime](https://www.bairdmaritime.com/shipping/taipei-chinas-traffic-control-order-for-taiwan-strait-during-typhoon-ridiculous)). The data are CC BY 4.0, and the free API is for non-commercial use, which matches aseri's non-commercial status ([licence](https://open-meteo.com/en/licence); [pricing](https://open-meteo.com/en/pricing)).

**Gap-filling rows** are the third exception, and they are untested. On the forecast day, the first steps of a lagging target have already happened. A fresher source can be passed as a known-future row: observed values for those gap days, then NaN, which Timestral masks ([catalogue](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/catalogue.md)). The notes proposed this for GPR's six stale days between Monday updates. Spec 6.2 limits it, though. A forecast is issued only when a series' reference date moves ([spec 6.2](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#62-forecasting)), so GPR and Brent are close to fresh when their live forecast goes out. The design therefore matters mainly for the four PortWatch series and their roughly four-day lag. Fresh GDELT or Wikipedia rows could cover days 1 to 3 there. It also matters for the alert series: at 07:00 UTC the daily run already sees seven hours of the UTC day that spec 6.1 drops as partial. That last idea is my inference, not a finding of the notes. Either way, the effect can reach only the first few steps of the 1-7 bucket.

The free data landscape narrows further on licences and access. EIA stopped publishing NYMEX futures after 2024-04-05, so no free oil futures curve remains ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/pet_pri_fut_s1_d.htm)). Futures were less accurate than no-change in mean squared error anyway ([Alquist, Kilian and Vigfusson](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2011/1022/ifdp1022.htm)). `pytrends` is archived, and the official Trends API alpha is gated ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-opens-alpha-testing-for-new-trends-api-targeting-developers-and-journalists/)). Repeated pulls return contradictory values ([Behnen et al. 2020](https://imada.sdu.dk/Research/EDML/2020/EDML20_paper_5.pdf)). CBOE volatility indices on FRED are copyrighted ([FRED OVX](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/OVXCLS)). The daily EPU index carries both a "copyrighted" notice and a "Public Domain: Citation Requested" tag ([FRED EPU](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/USEPUINDXD)). The Kaggle Air Force launch dataset is CC BY-NC-SA, with some missile types unreported since 2026-08-10 ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/view/piterfm/massive-missile-attacks-on-ukraine)). DeepStateMap needs prior approval ([licence](https://deepstatemap.live/license-en.html)). Freight indices are by subscription ([Marine Log](https://www.marinelog.com/news/baltic-exchange-and-freightos-launch-container-shipping-index/)). JMIC incident data come only as PDFs, with no licence found. Wikimedia (CC0), GDELT (free with citation), GPR (CC BY, with archived daily vintages) and PortWatch (attribution, non-commercial) remain clean ([catalogue, source register](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/catalogue.md)). Devetak's PortWatch test adds a practical warning. Feeding every other port and chokepoint to Timestral as context "barely changes forecast accuracy" and took up to about 15 minutes of CPU per forecast ([Devetak](https://devetak.github.io/data/timestral_blog.html)).

## Where this corrects aseri's earlier research

The earlier documents reached the right headline: keep the daily job univariate and let calendars through the scout. The new evidence keeps that headline. It corrects the statistics behind the earlier verdicts and several rules in spec 6.5. The most consequential correction is about overlap. A simulation in which neighbouring origins share target days shows how often each test rejects a true null at a nominal 5% ([scout_power_sim.py](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/code/scout_power_sim.py), 4,000 replications; rerun on 2026-10-30 with identical output). The iid test stands in for a plain bootstrap over origins, which is what the spike used and spec 6.5 prescribes.

| Regime persistence φ | Origins | Effective n | Naive iid | DM with HLN | EWC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.7 | 52 weekly | 16.9 | 0.18 | 0.08 | 0.05 |
| 0.95 | 52 weekly | 8.1 | 0.28 | 0.15 | 0.08 |
| 0.7 | 365 daily | 17.4 | 0.37 | 0.09 | 0.10 |
| 0.95 | 365 daily | 8.2 | 0.42 | 0.16 | 0.19 |

The table also shows that **365 daily origins carry no more information than 52 weekly ones** (effective n of about 17 in both cases). The corrections below follow from this result and from the rest of the evidence.

The Best practice report's broader findings still stand: the as-of store, the scoring vintage and the coverage arithmetic ([Best practice previsioni probabilistiche.md](Best%20practice%20previsioni%20probabilistiche.md)). The scout depends on its as-of store in particular.

## (a) Verdict: right about known futures, wrong as a rule for aseri

The impression is wrong as a general rule, and wrong for aseri's data in particular. Adding covariates improves a forecast only when each one tells the model something about the target's future that the target's own past does not, and only when that information is available on the forecast day. Significance in-sample establishes neither. It says a link exists in the population, not that this model, at this lag, will forecast better. The evidence runs one way. On fev-bench, covariates lower error by 10 to 13% within a model, all of it from known-future drivers, and the advantage shrinks as more covariates are added. Macro forecasters beat raw predictor stacking by compressing or pre-screening. Google Flu Trends overfit precisely by keeping significant terms. Conflict forecasting's best live model is a replay of each country's own past. aseri's spike found no covariate set worth keeping and several that moved forecasts the wrong way. The owner's intuition is right in one corner: a covariate whose future is known and that drives the target mechanically can cut error by 10 to 50%. aseri has few of those. The realistic outcome of a rigorous scout is that most series stay univariate. At most a calendar or a weather-forecast row might survive, for Taiwan, the Red Sea pair, or the 7-day Ukraine series. That is still worth knowing, and the scout's job is to establish it cheaply and honestly.

## (c) Scout protocol: ten steps from pre-registration to a human decision

The protocol is one config-driven, cached pipeline run monthly in `scout.yml`. It never touches the daily job.

### Step 1: Pre-register candidates, metric and rule before any run

Commit a config before each cohort of candidates. For each series, list at most about 15 candidates. Each one needs a written mechanism, an expected lead, a kind (calendar, forecast vintage, gap-filling or past-only), its lag mask and its **primary buckets**: the horizons where its mechanism should act. A weather row for Taiwan claims only days 1-7, and a shifted holiday for Suez claims days 8-28. Lags, transforms and sets each count as separate candidates. Sets hold at most three members, each set is one candidate, and greedy forward selection is banned because its final p-value is invalid. Each candidate must also pass a licence check and an as-of availability check. Add three placebos per series and the spike's positive control (Step 10). The primary metric is relative WIS on 21 levels, a ratio of means over identical (origin, horizon) pairs, per bucket. Fix α, the minimum detectable effect (5%), the point floor (2%), the windows, the origin weekday and the context length in the same file. The context length should equal the daily job's. A fixed-length window keeps Giacomini and White's assumptions literal ([McCracken 2020](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2020/2020-050.pdf)), but the scout should test what would be deployed. Any change is a commit with a reason, as [spec 6.5](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#65-covariate-scout) already requires.

Screen with Timestral-28M and confirm survivors with 209M. The spike found that the two sizes agreed on all 16 verdicts, but treating 28M as a proxy is an assumption that each report must state. Run Chronos-2 once on the calendar and weather candidates. If it gains where Timestral does not, the null is a model question, not a data question. Switching models restarts the live record and lies outside the scout.

### Step 3: Make every input as-of, and enforce five leakage rules in code

Every stored value carries `available_at`. `get_covariate(name, origin)` returns only rows available by the origin, in the vintage current then. Where no vintage exists, it falls back to the worst-case lag mask and flags the run `pseudo_real_time`. Croushore and Stark showed that latest-vintage errors "can appear deceptively lower" ([Philadelphia Fed WP 01-10](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/2001/wp01-10.pdf?sc_lang=en)). GPR's archived daily vintages make a true as-of test possible for that series ([GPR archive](https://github.com/iacoviel/iacoviel.github.io/tree/master/gpr_archive_files)).

The five rules follow, in order of likely damage. First, event data are aggregated by the time they were added, never by event date. GDELT's `DATEADDED` is the right clock, because its Mentions table records mentions "irrespective of the date of the original event" ([GDELT codebook](http://data.gdeltproject.org/documentation/GDELT-Event_Codebook-V2.0.pdf)). Second, revised series are read from stored vintages. Third, only pure functions of the date, registered forecast vintages (Open-Meteo Previous Runs keyed by issue time) and registered gap-filling rows may enter as known-future; any other request raises. This matters doubly for Timestral, because it scales known-future rows with whole-row statistics, so an oracle row leaks even through its scaling ([spike](../spike-covariates.md)). Fourth, lags, rolling means and z-scores are computed after masking, from as-of data only. Fifth, every source carries an availability timestamp, not just a date, to handle UTC alert days and the same-day drones report. The mask table lives in config and is re-measured each run as the worst lag seen over recent fetches. Origins follow the daily job's real issue schedule: a forecast only when the reference date moves, and trading days for Brent ([spec 6.2](../../superpowers/specs/2026-10-29-aseri-design.md#62-forecasting)).

### Step 4: Split time into discovery, embargo and a sealed holdout

Use weekly origins on a fixed weekday, rolling-origin only. K-fold cross-validation needs uncorrelated errors from a refitted model ([Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:csdana:v:120:y:2018:i:c:p:70-83)), and neither holds here. Discovery runs from a pre-registered start date to the holdout start minus an **embargo equal to the series' horizon** (28, 14 or 7 days), so no discovery target falls inside a holdout window. The holdout is **the most recent 52 weekly origins, sealed**. The code reads it only in Step 8, only for survivors, and logs each read. The split is fixed in config, because a split chosen after looking can "more than triple" rejection rates ([Hansen and Timmermann](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/12/rp12_43.pdf)).

Reasonable discovery starts are 2019 for PortWatch (about 340 discovery origins) and 2023 for alerts and drones. Weather candidates must start in January 2024, which leaves about 90 discovery origins. GPR can start at the same 2019 date to bound compute. Report the gain separately for older origins, which are possibly in Timestral's training data, and recent ones. A one-time backfill of 9 series × 15 candidates × 150 origins on 28M is about 2.3 CPU hours. Cache forecasts by (series, candidate, origin, model, context length, data-vintage hash), so monthly runs only add about four origins.

### Step 5: Size the test against effective sample size, not origin count

With weekly origins, the 8-28 bucket carries about a third of its origins as independent information: **an effective n of about 17 for 52 origins**. Daily origins add compute, not information. The 1-7 bucket of weekly origins does not overlap, so its effective n is close to n unless regimes are very persistent. For one-sided 80% power, n_eff ≈ (z(1-α) + 0.84)²·(σ/μ)², which is 6.2·(σ/μ)² at α = 0.05 and 15.4·(σ/μ)² at α = 0.001. Here μ is the relative WIS gain and σ the per-origin standard deviation of the relative differential. Back-solving the spike's intervals gives σ of about 2 to 6%; plan for 5 to 10%. The simulation's power at 52 weekly origins and φ = 0.7 confirms the formula: 0.60 to 0.73 for a 5% gain with σ = 10%, against 0.66 predicted ([scout_power_sim.py](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/code/scout_power_sim.py)).

| True gain μ | σ | Weekly origins, single test at α = 0.05 | Weekly origins, Bonferroni for 50 candidates |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | 5 to 10% | 10 to 20 | 25 to 50 |
| 5% | 5% | about 20 | about 50 |
| 5% | 10% | about 75 | about 185 (3.5 years) |
| 2% | 5% | about 115 (2.2 years) | about 290 (5.6 years) |

Double these for very persistent regimes. Alerts and drones have about 240 weekly origins in all, and PortWatch about 400. So **a 2% gain cannot be detected after multiplicity correction** without reaching into contaminated history. The rule is a 5% minimum detectable effect at the screen, with 2% kept as the point floor for adoption. Estimate n_eff per series from the autocorrelations of the discovery loss differentials, and write it into each report.

### Step 6: Score paired WIS per origin and bucket

Use the in-house 21-level WIS that the Best practice report specifies, tested against scoringutils' worked example ([Best practice report](Best%20practice%20previsioni%20probabilistiche.md)). For each origin and bucket, the differential is d = WIS(baseline) - WIS(candidate). The report shows the relative gain Σd/ΣWIS(baseline) with a stationary block-bootstrap interval, using blocks of at least 8 weekly origins or `arch.bootstrap.optimal_block_length` ([arch docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/timeseries-bootstraps.html)). Never use an iid bootstrap.

### Step 7: Screen discovery with HAR tests, Benjamini-Hochberg and StepM

For each candidate and primary bucket, run a one-sided t-test with the equal-weighted cosine long-run variance: ν = 0.4·n^(2/3) cosine terms and t_ν critical values ([Lazarus, Lewis, Stock and Watson](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/stock/files/lazarus_lewis_stock_watson_har_inference_recommendations_for_practice_jbes_2018.pdf)). Cross-check it with DM-HLN using k - 1 lags, where k = ⌈bucket's maximum horizon / 7⌉ (4 for days 8-28, 1 and so no lags for days 1-7). In statsmodels 0.15 that means `diebold_mariano_test(..., lags=k-1, harvey_adj=True)`, with precomputed WIS passed through a callable criterion. The returned p-value is two-sided, so halve it ([statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.tsa.stattools.diebold_mariano_test.html)). A candidate's p-value is the largest across its primary buckets, an intersection-union test that needs no correction across buckets ([arXiv 2202.12540](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12540)).

Apply Benjamini-Hochberg at q = 0.10 over all candidates in the run. That is valid because every candidate faces the same univariate control, the "many treatments with a single control" case that Benjamini and Yekutieli cover. Use their BY variant if series are pooled ([Annals of Statistics 2001](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.aos/1013699998)). Gate each series with Hansen's SPA consistent p-value ([Hansen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500105000000063)). If it is above 0.10, report "none" for that series and stop there. In parallel, run Romano-Wolf StepM at size 0.10, studentized, with a stationary bootstrap and an explicit block of 8 and a seed ([Romano and Wolf 2005](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/browse/2005/07/01/stepwise-multiple-testing-formalized-data-snooping); [arch StepM](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/multiple-comparison/generated/arch.bootstrap.StepM.html)). arch's default block of √T is wrong for this design. Survivors are the BH discoveries that StepM also flags, at most 3 per series and about 10 in all. White's Reality Check is superseded by SPA. The Model Confidence Set answers a different question ("which models tie for best") and is a secondary output only. In the simulation, StepM recovered exactly the 3 planted 5% gains among 40 candidates at 104 origins, in one draw.

### Step 8: Confirm survivors once, on the holdout, with Holm

On the sealed holdout, rerun the one-sided EWC test on each primary bucket, apply the intersection-union rule, then Holm at 0.05 across all survivors. A survivor is adopted only if four more conditions hold. Its point gain must be at least 2% on each primary bucket. No other bucket may be significantly worse, at one-sided 10%. Its cov80 must fall inside the binomial band for the bucket's effective n, not a fixed 0.70 to 0.90. And it must pass Step 9. A candidate that fails is not re-tested until 26 new weekly origins exist, and the re-test uses only those as its holdout. Re-checking every month on mostly the same origins is optional stopping, which makes p-values "wholly unreliable" ([Johari, Pekelis and Walsh](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922)). Always-valid sequential tests are the alternative if monthly monitoring is ever wanted, at more machinery than one maintainer needs.

### Step 9: Require stability across periods and events

Split discovery and holdout together into at least 3 disjoint periods, such as calendar years. The point gain must be positive in each, with no period significantly negative at one-sided 10%. Run the Giacomini-Rossi fluctuation test on the rolling relative WIS, with a window of 0.3 to 0.5 of the sample, and treat a crossing of the lower critical value as a veto ([Giacomini and Rossi 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:jae:japmet:v:25:y:2010:i:4:p:595-620)). No Python implementation exists. Port it from R's `murphydiagram::fluctuation_test` and take the critical values from the paper's Table 1, which the research did not retrieve ([rdrr.io](https://rdrr.io/cran/murphydiagram/man/fluctuation.html)). For event-type covariates, count events, not origins: **adopt nothing on fewer than 2 independent events**. Lunar New Year has 8. The Hormuz collapse has 1, so no Hormuz regime covariate can pass yet. A rule that applies only during shocks is a new model. It must be pre-registered as its own candidate, never fitted afterwards. Giacomini and White's conditional test is at most a diagnostic, because its null is "extremely narrow" ([McCracken 2020](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2020/2020-050.pdf)).

### Step 10: Prune, shadow, approve and report, with tests that validate the protocol

For an adopted set, compute leave-one-covariate-out gains and drop any member whose gain is not positive. A redundant member is pure failure-point cost ([Lei et al., LOCO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.04173)). Do not use permutation importance. It forces the model to extrapolate on dependent features and breaks temporal structure ([Hooker, Mentch and Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03151)). Run the passing configuration in shadow beside the live job for at least 12 weeks. Shadow data are the only data free of training contamination, but 12 weeks is a sanity gate (the sign holds, coverage does not collapse), not a significance test. A human then approves, and the model ID suffix changes as spec 6.5 requires. Each run writes `docs/research/scout/{date}.md` with every candidate's gain, interval, raw and adjusted p-values, the stage reached and the reason it stopped, plus the placebo pass rate. Failures are results.

The protocol is only as good as its own checks. These tests run on fixtures in CI, plus end to end once per release.

| Test | What it does | Passes when |
|---|---|---|
| Size under overlap | Simulated overlapping differentials with zero true gain, φ = 0.7, 52 weekly origins | The scout's test rejects at most 8% at nominal 5%, and the naive test is shown to over-reject (about 18%); with no overlap, every test holds size |
| Planted-effect power | 5% gains planted in 3 of 40 candidates, σ = 5%, 104 weekly origins | Stage 1 flags exactly the planted candidates |
| Placebos, every run | Per series: iid noise, the real covariate shifted by ±1 year, a block-permuted copy, and for calendars the dates shifted by several weeks | No placebo passes Stage 2, and the Stage 1 placebo pass rate stays near or below q |
| Positive control | The spike's synthetic 5-day lead (`control.py`), which cut 209M's error by 89% | It passes every stage |
| Leak canary | The target shifted forward, requested as a covariate | The as-of layer raises; with the guard bypassed, the gain is huge |
| As-of property | Random origins | The maximum `available_at` fed to the model is at most the origin, every known-future row is a pure function of the date or a registered vintage, and GDELT aggregates change only as `DATEADDED` allows |
| Reproducibility | No-covariate run, then a rerun of a month | Bit-identical to the cached baseline (as in the spike), and identical decisions from the same cache and config |
| Scoring | WIS and DM implementations | WIS equals scoringutils' example; DM equals statsmodels 0.15 with `harvey_adj=True` (matched to 1e-14 in the notes) |

The thresholds in these tests (8% size, 12-week shadow, 3 sub-periods, 2 events) are proposed defaults, not values taken from a source. The protocol also rests on an untested assumption: no paper derives DM or Giacomini-White asymptotics for frozen in-context forecasters, so treating Timestral as a method with no estimated parameters is an inference ([methodology notes](../research_notes/Covariate%20per%20le%20previsioni%20aseri/methodology.md)). Both are reasons to run the size and placebo tests on every release, not once.
