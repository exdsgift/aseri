# Visualizing probabilistic time-series forecasts for non-expert decision makers (aseri forecast chart)

Scope note for the report writer. "Empirical" marks a controlled study or measured result; "guidance" marks a standard, style guide, institutional practice or expert opinion. Researched 2026-10-30.

How the current chart produces the complaint, read from the code (no source needed, these are the repo's own files):
- `web/src/lib/chart.ts`, `paintChart`: the gold 7-day-average line ends at `averages[origin]` and a 3x3 gold square marks that point as "now" (`square(plot, xo - 1, y(averages[origin]) - 1, 3, color.gold)`), while the median polyline and every band start at the raw daily value `last = values[origin]` (`forecastAt(last, ...)`, `[xo, y(last)]`). So the origin column carries two different "now" points (181 and 165 in the user's example), and the brighter, marked one is not where the forecast starts.
- The daily values are 1 to 2 art-pixel dots in `--muted-foreground` (#9badb7), the 7-day average is a solid gold line: the eye reads the line as "the series", then the forecast continues a different quantity (raw daily, with a weekend sawtooth).
- Spec 6.2 fixes the target as the raw daily value and says the 7-day average is "display context only, never forecast separately"; spec 6.8 puts the GPR 7-day average as the giant hero number next to a single-day forecast sentence.
- Measured WCAG contrast of the spec 6.9 tokens against the background #071324 (computed with the WCAG 2 relative-luminance formula): gold #ffd23f 12.9:1, pale gold #fff0b3 16.3:1, muted gold #917d3c 4.6:1, label grey #9badb7 8.0:1, frame #253a56 1.6:1, focus #5b6ee1 4.2:1. Between marks: pale gold vs gold 1.26:1 (the median line and the 7-day line are nearly the same lightness), white vs label grey 2.3:1 (past vs replayed real values).

## 1. Continuity between history and forecast: where should the forecast start?

### Takeaway
There is no single industry convention, and I found no controlled study of "connect vs do not connect". What the strong practices share is that the history and the forecast are the same quantity, so wherever the forecast starts, it starts from (or next to) the last observation of that quantity. aseri's problem is not the anchor point itself (the last daily value is a legitimate anchor for a daily forecast) but that the history's dominant mark and "now" marker are a different quantity.

### Cited Findings
- Bank of England fan chart (guidance, the origin of the format): the forecast has been published as a probability distribution since February 1996; the earlier chart, with a central line and symmetric bands, "encouraged the reader to concentrate on an apparently precise central projection", and the fan's objective was "to focus attention on the whole of the forecast distribution, rather than on small changes to the central projection": [Britton, Fisher and Whitley 1998, BoE Quarterly Bulletin](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/1998/q1/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart) ([PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1998/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart)).
- Same article: bands are built outward from the mode in 10% probability steps until 90% is covered; the central band "includes the central projection: there is judged to be a 10% chance that inflation will be within that central band"; uncertainty grows with horizon, "much less certainty about the outcome two years ahead than about the outcome one quarter ahead ... shown by the widening of the bands": [BoE 1998 PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1998/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart).
- The BoE's history line and fan are the same measure (twelve-month RPIX inflation, "Percentage increase in prices on a year earlier") on one axis: [BoE 1998 PDF, Chart 1 axis label](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1998/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart).
- The BoE GDP fan chart does not pinch to a point at the last data: since 2007 it extends backwards as a "backcast", because "the distribution to the left of the vertical dashed line reflects the likelihood of revisions to the data over the past" (guidance): [BoE fan charts, Aug 2017](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/inflation-report/2017/fan-charts-aug-2017.pdf); announced in [BoE 2007 speech "Risk, uncertainty and monetary policy"](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2007/risk-uncertainty-and-monetary-policy). Galvão and Mitchell find these past-data uncertainties "can be accurately quantified, even without judgement, using past revisions data" (empirical): [ESCoE DP 2019-08](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nsr/escoed/escoe-dp-2019-08.html).
- Hubverse `hubVis::plot_step_ahead_model_output` (guidance, the reference viz for Hubverse model output, which aseri publishes): plots model-output intervals (default `intervals = c(0.5, 0.8, 0.95)`, fill transparency 0.25) against `target_data` of the same target; it documents no connecting segment, and in the reference example the forecast ribbons start at the first forecast `target_date` and overlap the observed series rather than joining it at one point: [hubVis reference](https://hubverse-org.github.io/hubVis/reference/plot_step_ahead_model_output.html).
- fable/fpp3 (guidance): forecasts are drawn with the historical data via `autoplot()`, intervals as shaded regions whose shading "reflects the probability", 80% and 95% by default; the fpp3 text does not say whether the point forecast joins the last observation: [fpp3, Prediction intervals](https://otexts.com/fpp3/prediction-intervals.html).
- Prophet (guidance, third-party description, not the official docs): observed days are black dots and one blue line carries both the in-sample fit and the forecast, with an 80% interval by default; so Prophet's visual continuity comes from the model's fitted line, not from the last observation: [r-statistics.co Prophet tutorial](https://r-statistics.co/Prophet-in-R.html); default interval width documented in [Prophet docs, Uncertainty intervals](https://facebook.github.io/prophet/docs/uncertainty_intervals.html).
- NHC cone (guidance): "The cone represents the probable track of the center of a tropical cyclone", built from circles along the forecast track at 12 to 120 h, each sized so that "two-thirds of historical official forecast errors over a 5-year sample fall within the circle" (2021-2025 sample on the current page): [NHC, Definition of the cone](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/aboutcone.shtml?text).
- People forecasting from graphs anchor on the last data point (or recent level) and under-adjust, producing "trend damping" (empirical): [Harvey and Reimers 2013, trend damping](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/13591); an adaptive-anchoring account models judgments as anchored on recent values (empirical): [Cognitive Science 2017, PMC5811814](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5811814/).
- The visual form of the history changes the forecasts lay people draw from it: in four web experiments with over 4,000 participants, forecasts were lower and noisier for bar charts than for line or point graphs (empirical): [Reimers and Harvey 2024, IJF 40(1)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v40y2024i1p44-61.html) ([open access](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/29623/)).
- Practitioner convention (guidance, opinion): a line switches from solid (actuals) to dashed (projection) at the origin, and the axis wording should make the actual/forecast split clear: [SAP Fiori chart guidance](https://experience.sap.com/fiori-design-web/?p=46035); [Storytelling with Data, "when to use a dotted line"](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2018/5/8/when-to-use-a-dotted-line).

### Inferences
- The reader's "current value" is whatever the history draws most prominently plus whatever the headline number says. In aseri both are the 7-day average (gold line, gold "now" square, giant hero number), so any forecast of the raw daily value looks like it "does not start from the current value", even though `forecastAt` anchors correctly on the last daily value. The fix is to make the anchor and the dominant history mark the same quantity (section 2), not to move the fan to the average.
- Because readers anchor on the last point (Harvey and Reimers), a fan that starts at a weekday-low or weekday-high daily value will be read as a jump if the last visible prominent mark is the smoother average.
- Pinching the bands to zero width at the origin claims the last value is exact. That is defensible for PortWatch counts, but GPR revises its recent values (spec 6.3); by the BoE backcast precedent the honest options are a small band on the last few days of history or not pinching (start the first band column at h=1).
- Interpolating quantiles linearly between day 0 and day 1 (`forecastAt`) draws values that no forecast states. For a daily series a per-day step (one band column per target day) is more truthful; fable and hubVis both draw only at forecast dates.

**Rules for the aseri chart, with checks**
1. One "now": the origin marker, the start of the median, the readout's "today" row and any number printed beside the chart refer to the same quantity and the same value. Check (unit test on `chart.ts`): for a fixture with `values[origin] = 165` and `averages[origin] = 181`, assert the origin marker's y equals the median polyline's first y, and that both equal `y(values[origin])` when the forecast target is daily. Review: cover the forecast half of the chart; the last prominent mark must equal the number in the readout.
2. Same series continues across the origin: the most prominent history mark must be the series being forecast (connected daily dots or a thin daily line), and the 7-day line, if kept, is visibly subordinate (thinner/dimmer/dashed) and labelled as an average. Check: screenshot review at narrow width; a reader asked "what value is the forecast continuing?" answers with the daily value.
3. Mark the forecast zone with a non-colour cue too (aseri already uses the hatch and the dotted origin line plus the "Next N days" label, consistent with the solid-to-dashed convention).
4. Do not pinch to zero width at the origin for series with known revisions (GPR); either start the bands at h=1 or widen the last observed days. Check: for `gpr_daily`, assert band width at the origin column is > 0 or that band drawing starts at the first target date.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled experiment comparing "forecast joined to last observation" vs "forecast starting at h=1 with a gap" for lay readers; the recommendations above rest on anchoring research and institutional practice.
- I did not retrieve primary documentation of how FiveThirtyEight's or the Economist's election models draw the origin, nor statsforecast's `plot` conventions; not verified, so no claim is made.
- The NHC definition page does not state where the cone starts or what it excludes; the "not storm size" caveat is sourced in section 4.
- I could not confirm whether the BoE still publishes fan charts in its 2025-2026 Monetary Policy Reports after the Bernanke review (section 3).

## 2. Showing the same quantity in history and forecast (7-day average vs raw daily, weekly seasonality)

### Takeaway
Mixing a smoothed history with a raw-value forecast is the root cause of the complaint. Dashboards that show both daily values and a 7-day average keep both in the history, and forecasting hubs forecast an aggregated target (weekly totals) and plot it against observations of that same target. Either show the daily series prominently on both sides, or forecast the 7-day average (or weekly total) as its own target; a 7-day-average forecast cannot be derived correctly from the daily marginal quantiles aseri archives.

### Cited Findings
- CDC COVID Data Tracker (guidance): daily cases as bars with a line for "the sum of cases over the last 7 days, divided by 7", averages used "to reduce reporting differences"; the average is a trailing window (current day plus 6 preceding days): [CDC archived tracker snapshot](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/102871/cdc_102871_DS1.pdf).
- The COVID Tracking Project (guidance from data-quality analysis): weekend reporting gaps make daily data dip, which "can seem like a sign that trends are improving"; catch-up days look like spikes that "usually [aren't] an indication of a sudden spike"; it recommends 7- and 14-day averages to "show the general trends": [COVID Tracking Project, day-of-week effects](https://covidtracking.com/analysis-updates/how-day-of-week-effects-impact-covid-19-data).
- Full Fact (guidance): cases are "often shown as a seven-day rolling average to avoid the artificial drop that appears at weekends": [Full Fact](https://fullfact.org/health/covid-cases-uk/).
- Forecast hubs aggregate the target instead of mixing: the European COVID-19 Forecast Hub says "Source data are reported daily, but we require weekly forecasts" and uses "the count of new hospitalisations (or cases/deaths) per week" (guidance): [European hub, targets and horizons](https://www.github.com/european-modelling-hubs/covid19-forecast-hub-europe/wiki/targets-and-horizons). The US hub's "wk" targets end on the Saturday of an epiweek, and the CDC successor hub collects weekly totals per epiweek: [US COVID-19 Forecast Hub, data-processed README](https://github.com/reichlab/covid19-forecast-hub/blob/master/data-processed/README.md).
- hubVis plots forecasts against `target_data`, a date and "observation" series for the same target, so history and forecast share a quantity by construction (guidance): [hubVis reference](https://hubverse-org.github.io/hubVis/reference/plot_step_ahead_model_output.html).
- Quantiles do not add up: fpp3 notes the point forecast is a mean partly because "medians don't add up" across aggregates while means do (guidance, statistical fact): [fpp3, Prediction intervals](https://otexts.com/fpp3/prediction-intervals.html). The same holds across days: the median of a 7-day average is not the average of the 7 daily medians, and its interval depends on the dependence between days, which marginal quantiles do not carry.
- Graph format of the history shifts lay extrapolation (lines and points vs bars) (empirical): [Reimers and Harvey 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/29623/).

### Inferences
- aseri currently does the CDC thing in the history (daily marks plus a 7-day line) but makes the average dominant, then forecasts only the daily value. The CDC pattern is a history-only pattern; no source I found extends a smoothed history line with a raw-value forecast.
- Two consistent designs exist:
  - **A. Daily on both sides (fits spec 6.2 as is).** Daily values become the primary history mark (connected dots or a thin line, as bright as the median), so the weekday sawtooth is visible before the origin and its continuation after the origin reads as continuation, not a crash. The 7-day line becomes a thin, dimmer context line that stops at the origin with a label "7-day avg". Weekend columns can carry a subtle background tint so the dips are explained by the axis.
  - **B. Average on both sides (needs a spec change).** Add a second Hubverse target, for example the trailing 7-day mean or weekly total, forecast directly (or from model sample paths), and plot it against the observed 7-day average. Averaging the daily quantiles to fake it is wrong for the bands (dependence ignored) and approximate even for the median.
- The headline mixes quantities in one breath (giant 7-day average, then a single-day forecast "4 weeks from the last data"). Spec 6.8 already compares same weekday with same weekday, which is right for a weekly cycle; the remaining fix is labelling: the giant number says "7-day average" on its face, and the sentence names "a single day" and shows the last value of that same weekday, never the average.
- Weekday cycles are the reason the COVID Tracking Project found readers misreading dips as trend changes; aseri's forecast weekend dip to about 104 from a 181 average is that misreading built into the forecast display.

**Rules for the aseri chart, with checks**
1. Every number or mark that is compared must be the same quantity: history line vs forecast median, hero number vs forecast sentence, card 7-day value vs card "In h days: lo-hi". Check (type test): give the chart's series a `quantity` tag ("daily" or "7-day average") and assert the primary history series and the forecast share it; in review, list every number on the page with its quantity and flag any adjacent pair that differs.
2. If design A: daily history mark contrast at least equal to the median line's; 7-day line visibly subordinate and ending at the origin. Check: snapshot plus the contrast test in section 6.
3. If design B: the average forecast comes from the model (or sample paths), never from averaging daily quantiles. Check: a unit test that fails if any code path computes band edges of an aggregate by averaging quantiles.
4. Show the weekly cycle where it lives: weekday ticks or weekend shading in the forecast zone, and the readout names the weekday ("Sat Nov 28"). Check: reader test asking "is the value expected to fall at the end of November?"; correct answer cites the weekend, not a trend.

### Gaps
- I found no Our World in Data methods page that states its rationale for 7-day averages; the rationale is sourced from CDC, the COVID Tracking Project and Full Fact instead.
- I found no empirical study of the specific error "smoothed history line + raw forecast"; the risk is inferred from anchoring research and the day-of-week misreading documented by the COVID Tracking Project.
- The CDC tracker description comes from archived 2021 snapshots; the current interface was not checked.

## 3. Uncertainty displays: fan charts, interval bands, quantile dotplots, hypothetical outcome plots, frequency framing

### Takeaway
Showing uncertainty improves lay decisions and costs little trust. Among formats, frequency-based displays (quantile dotplots, hypothetical outcome plots, ensembles) give the best decisions in controlled studies; plain interval bands are acceptable when labelled with numbers and frequency words, and become worse when a prominent central line or mean is added. aseri's "8 in 10" and "5 in 10" wording matches the evidence; its pale-gold median line, the brightest mark in the forecast zone, does not.

### Cited Findings
- Uncertainty forecasts improved weather-related decisions and trust: participants took appropriate precautions and withheld unnecessary ones more often than with deterministic forecasts; the harm from forecast error was attenuated; categorical advice alone did not help (empirical): [Joslyn and LeClerc 2012, J Exp Psych: Applied 18(1)](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/joslyn:2012:UFID/index.html).
- Numeric ranges barely cost trust: across five UK surveys (n = 5,780), a numerical range slightly reduced trust in the number and not significantly in the source; a verbal caveat cost more (empirical): [van der Bles et al. 2020, PNAS (summary)](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200323152106.htm); [secondary summary with effect directions](https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/paper-summary-the-effects-of-communicating).
- UK GDP experiments: quantitative uncertainty formats (intervals, density strips, bell curves) helped the public understand revisions without reducing trust, and helped align subjective with objective probabilities (empirical): [Galvão and Mitchell, Cleveland Fed WP 21-28R](https://www.clevelandfed.org/en/newsroom-and-events/publications/working-papers/2022-working-papers/wp-2128r-communicating-data-uncertainty-multi-wave-experimental-evidence-for-uk-gdp.aspx).
- Quantile dotplots and CDF plots gave the best decisions in an incentivized bus-catching task; dotplots with 50 outcomes reached about 97% of optimal payoff, 5 points above control, more consistent across people; textual uncertainty was sensitive to which interval it stated (empirical): [Fernandes et al. 2018, CHI](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2018-UncertainBusDecisions-CHI.pdf) ([project page](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/uncertainty-bus)).
- Quantile dotplots reduced the variance of probability estimates about 1.15 times vs density plots, and people estimated with more confidence; they turn percentiles into counting (empirical): [Kay et al. 2016, "When(ish) is my bus?", CHI](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/when-ish-is-my-bus); [method note](https://github.com/mjskay/when-ish-is-my-bus/blob/master/quantile-dotplots.md).
- Hypothetical outcome plots (animated draws) gave much more accurate judgments than error bars and violin plots for two or three quantities, similar for one (empirical): [Hullman, Resnick and Adar 2015, PLOS ONE](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/hops). For time series, observers identified the true trend from weaker evidence with HOPs than with error bars or line ensembles (empirical): [Kale et al. 2019, TVCG 25(1)](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops-trends).
- Adding means to uncertainty displays produced small biasing effects on estimates and decisions, linked to users discounting uncertainty; designs best for unbiased estimates were not best for decisions (empirical, pre-registered): [Kale, Kay and Hullman 2021, TVCG 27(2)](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/2020-vis-effect-size-judgements).
- Error bars cause a "within-the-bar" bias and a binary inside/outside reading that overstates effects; gradient plots (opacity) and violin plots avoid it (empirical): [Correll and Gleicher 2014, TVCG](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf).
- Summary displays (the cone) were misread as size; ensembles evoked accurate readings of the distribution but people overweighted individual members near a point of interest (empirical): [Padilla, Ruginski and Creem-Regehr 2017, Cognitive Research](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5626802/).
- COVID forecast displays (two experiments, N = 2,549, 34 techniques from the COVID-19 Forecast Hub): showing six or more models raised risk estimates most; cumulative scales consistently raised perceived risk (empirical): [Padilla et al. 2022, Scientific Reports](https://vis.khoury.northeastern.edu/pubs/Padilla2022ImpactCOVID19Forecast). Trust was highest for displays with less information (a 95% interval, a single forecast, grayscale), but the 95% interval was the most likely to evoke predictions not matching the actual trend (empirical): [Padilla et al. 2023, TVCG 29(1), "Multiple Forecast Visualizations"](https://vis.khoury.northeastern.edu/pubs/Padilla2022MultipleForecastVisualizations).
- Temperature-range displays: identical relative positions within a range were judged differently depending on range width; the internal model of the distribution related to numeracy (empirical): [Tak, Toet and van Erp 2015, JCEDM](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/public-understanding-of-visual-representations-of-uncertainty-in-/).
- Spiegelhalter, Pearson and Short (review, guidance): natural frequencies ("1 in 10") help over percentages; give frequencies "with a clearly defined denominator of constant size", with and without the outcome; "Assume low numeracy of a general public audience and adopt a less-is-more approach"; "Using shading to represent probability is unsuitable for fine quantitative comparisons"; the BoE "has always refused to include a numerical central estimate when first releasing the graphic"; "there is limited experimental evidence" on how visualizations are understood: [Science 333:1393, 2011](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/spiegelhalter_visualizing.pdf).
- Padilla, Kay and Hullman's review chapter treats frequency framing (icon arrays, quantile dotplots, HOPs) as a central, evidence-backed principle (guidance; summary via course slides, chapter itself not retrieved): [Padilla, Kay and Hullman 2021, "Uncertainty Visualization", Wiley StatsRef, DOI 10.1002/9781118445112.stat08296](https://friendly.github.io/6135/lectures/Uncertainty.pdf).
- fpp3: "point forecasts can be of almost no value without the accompanying prediction intervals" (guidance): [fpp3](https://otexts.com/fpp3/prediction-intervals.html).
- Bernanke review of BoE forecasting (guidance, 2024): Recommendation 11 says the MPR fan charts "have weak conceptual foundations", "convey little useful information over and above what could be communicated in other, more direct ways", "receive little attention from the public" and "should be eliminated"; instead a section on uncertainty that could include the Bank's forecast error record, plus published alternative scenarios (Recommendation 8): [BoE, Forecasting for monetary policy making and communication: a review](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/independent-evaluation-office/forecasting-for-monetary-policy-making-and-communication-at-the-bank-of-england-a-review/forecasting-for-monetary-policy-making-and-communication-at-the-bank-of-england-a-review). Note: the critique targets the BoE's judgment-built fans, not model quantiles.
- Survey of evaluation practice: across 86 papers, uncertainty-visualization evaluations lean on performance and satisfaction measures that assume more statistically driven judgment than decision research supports (guidance from a systematic review): [Hullman et al. 2019, "In Pursuit of Error", TVCG 25(1)](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/uncertainty-eval-survey).

### Inferences
- aseri's bands with "5 in 10" and "8 in 10" are a reasonable static default; the dither (opacity-like) bands follow Correll and Gleicher's gradient idea, but the solid band edges re-introduce a hard inside/outside boundary. Keep edges subtle or drop them on the outer band.
- The pale-gold median (16.3:1, the brightest thing in the forecast zone) plus the 12 px pale end label make the median the anchor; Kale 2021 and the BoE practice argue for demoting it: dimmer or dotted median, end labels showing the range first ("8 in 10: lo-hi", then "most likely m").
- A quantile dotplot is the best-supported addition for the decision moment (one chosen day): 20 dots, each "1 in 20", in the intervals block or the hover tip. With aseri's archived quantile levels this needs evenly spaced levels; dots at 0.025, 0.075, ..., 0.975 can be interpolated from the archived quantiles (an approximation, label it).
- HOPs or spaghetti lines need joint sample paths (Hubverse supports a `sample` output type); with marginal quantiles only, any drawn trajectory would be invented, so HOPs are out unless the pipeline saves samples. Note also Padilla 2017: individual lines get overweighted.
- Frequency wording should keep a constant denominator ("8 in 10", "5 in 10", never "4 in 5"), and state the complement ("2 in 10 days land outside this range").

**Rules for the aseri chart, with checks**
1. Always show at least one interval with the number range in text; never a median alone (cards: "In h days: lo-hi" already does this). Check: unit test that every rendered forecast label with a median also renders the 8-in-10 range.
2. Demote the median: lower contrast than the band edges or the daily history marks; end-of-chart labels list the range at least as prominently as the median. Check: contrast test asserts median contrast <= daily-mark contrast; review that the largest forecast number on the page is not a bare median.
3. Frequency words with a fixed denominator of 10; "most likely", never "will" (spec 6.4 already). Check: a text lint over the copy (grep for "will", "%" next to "chance", "confidence interval").
4. Optional, best-evidenced upgrade: a 20-dot quantile dotplot for the selected day. Check: dot positions equal the interpolated quantiles in a unit test; reader task "pick a value you would be 9 in 10 sure to stay under" answered by counting.

### Gaps
- No study I found tests dithered pixel-art bands specifically; the closest evidence is gradient vs error bars (Correll and Gleicher) and Reimers and Harvey's null effect of shading intensity on point forecasts.
- I did not retrieve the full Padilla, Kay and Hullman chapter or the full van der Bles paper; effect sizes above come from abstracts and summaries.
- Whether the BoE actually dropped fan charts after the 2024 review was not confirmed.

## 4. Known misreadings and how to reduce them

### Takeaway
Three misreadings are well documented: the deterministic construal error (reading an interval as a deterministic range of values, for example a daily high and low), the cone-as-size error (reading widening as growth), and anchoring on the most salient point or line. Explicit text that states what the graphic is and is not, frequency phrasing including the complement, and de-emphasising the central line reduce them; keys and legends alone do not.

### Cited Findings
- Deterministic construal error, defined: viewers "interpret the image as representing some deterministic quantity" (review, guidance with empirical citations): [Joslyn and Savelli 2021, Frontiers in Computer Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full).
- In Savelli and Joslyn 2013, 36% of participants read the bounds of a predictive-interval graphic as daytime high and nighttime low; with the same interval in text, "Nearly everyone (94%) was able to correctly identify the numbers intended"; a prominent key, broken or blurred lines and a connecting line did not prevent the error (empirical, reported in the review): [Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full); original: [Savelli and Joslyn 2013, Applied Cognitive Psychology](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.2932).
- Designer recommendations from the same review (guidance): state both outcomes; explain what the graphic does not show (telling viewers the cone width "tells you nothing about the size or intensity of the storm" reduced that impression, Boone et al. 2018); avoid emphasising medians, which "may inappropriately withdraw attention from the uncertainty" (Fundel et al. 2019); test with open-ended questions before revealing that the graphic shows uncertainty: [Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full).
- Cone-as-size: novices read the cone as a storm growing in size and intensity (empirical): [Padilla et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5626802/); different cone variants changed decisions about storm characteristics (empirical): [Ruginski et al. 2016, Spatial Cognition and Computation](https://escholarship.org/content/qt96s9t7g5/qt96s9t7g5.pdf?t=sggz3w).
- NWS/NHC caveats (guidance): "The Track Forecast Cone simply communicates the most likely track of the center"; "Actual impacts on land after landfall can extend hundreds of miles outside the cone in all directions"; "The center of a tropical cyclone historically moves out of the cone about a third of the time"; NHC adds the current wind-field size to the graphic so that size is shown separately from track uncertainty: [NWS, Jamie Rhome on the cone (2022)](https://www.weather.gov/news/101722-jamie-rhome-cone); coverage is two-thirds of historical errors: [NHC](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/aboutcone.shtml?text). The wording "does not show the size of the storm. Hazardous conditions can occur outside of the cone" appears on NWS storm maps per news coverage I did not verify against an NHC page.
- Anchoring on the last point and under-adjusting in judgmental forecasts from graphs (empirical): [Harvey and Reimers 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/13591).
- Means in uncertainty displays bias judgments (empirical): [Kale, Kay and Hullman 2021](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/2020-vis-effect-size-judgements). The BoE's pre-1996 chart "encouraged the reader to concentrate on an apparently precise central projection" (guidance): [BoE 1998](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1998/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart).
- 90% is not 100%: Spiegelhalter notes that after the November 2007 BoE GDP fan chart "the true series subsequently plummeted out of the 90% interval and off the bottom of the chart" (guidance/case): [Spiegelhalter et al. 2011](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/spiegelhalter_visualizing.pdf).
- Range width changes perceived probabilities nonlinearly (empirical): [Tak et al. 2015](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/public-understanding-of-visual-representations-of-uncertainty-in-/).

### Inferences
- aseri-specific DCE risks: (a) the 8-in-10 band edges read as "highest and lowest values the series will reach" or as the weekday swing; (b) the widening fan read as "the series will get more volatile" (the cone-as-size analogue); (c) the weekend sawtooth of the median read as a forecast collapse; (d) the 28-day median end label read as "the value on day 28".
- Because legends and keys do not fix the DCE (Savelli and Joslyn), the fix is real text next to the chart, which aseri's readout and intervals block already provide; the copy should say what the band is and is not.
- Suggested copy, built from the cited mitigations (to be tested, not itself evidenced):
  - Under the chart: "Shaded: where the daily value should land 8 days in 10 (outer) and 5 in 10 (inner). It widens because later days are harder to call, not because the value will swing more. About 2 days in 10 should land outside."
  - Next to weekend dips: "Weekends are usually lower: compare Saturday with Saturday."
  - Readout order: range first, then "most likely".
- Two-thirds (NHC) vs 8 in 10 (aseri) shows that the coverage level is a design choice; aseri should state its level wherever the band appears (it does, via the labels).

**Rules for the aseri chart, with checks**
1. Text beside the chart states what the band means, what it does not mean (not a high/low, not volatility), and the complement ("2 in 10 outside"). Check: content review against this list; reader test with an open-ended "what does the shaded area show?" before any explanation (Joslyn and Savelli's recommended protocol), pass if most answers mention chance/likelihood rather than high/low.
2. No single forecast number appears without its range anywhere on the page (cards, hero, tooltips, OG image). Check: grep the rendered HTML/text for "Most likely" not followed by an "8 in 10" range in the same block.
3. Weekly-cycle explanation in text when the median has a weekly pattern. Check: reader task "will the value drop at the end of the month?"

### Gaps
- I found no study on misreadings of fan widening for economic or count time series specifically (the cone-as-size evidence is spatial); the analogy is an inference.
- The suggested copy has not been user-tested.

## 5. Showing track record and backtests honestly

### Takeaway
Retrospective (backfilled) forecasts look better than real-time ones, mainly through data revisions and hindsight in model choices, so they must be labelled, kept apart from the live record, and scored against the data vintage available at the time. Honest track records publish calibration (coverage vs the stated level) for all forecasts under a rule fixed in advance, with sample size and expected sampling noise.

### Cited Findings
- Retrospective setups leak information: a claimed AI forecasting gain over the CDC COVID-19 ensemble for 2024/25 is "attributable to information leakage in the retrospective forecasting setup" because data revisions were not taken into account; the authors warn this affects many forecasting fields (empirical/methodological, preprint Aug 2026): [Bracher and Funk 2026, arXiv:2608.05883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05883).
- Ensembles trained on revised data get an unfair advantage; retrospective ensembles should use only data available at the time (empirical): [Ray et al., arXiv:2201.12387](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.12387). The CMU Delphi supplement quantifies the effect of ignoring "what was known when" (empirical): [covidcast forecast supplement](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~ryantibs/papers/covidcast-forecast-supp.pdf).
- In macroeconomics, error measures can look deceptively low when computed on latest-available rather than real-time data vintages (empirical): [Croushore and Stark, Philadelphia Fed WP](https://fedinprint.org/item/fedpwp/4340/original).
- FiveThirtyEight's "Checking our work" publishes calibration plots for its forecasts back to 2008, binning stated probabilities against observed frequencies (a well-calibrated forecast lies near the 45-degree line), plus Brier skill scores, and states when results were not well calibrated (e.g., fewer upsets than expected) (guidance/practice): [FiveThirtyEight, Checking our work](https://projects.fivethirtyeight.com/checking-our-work); [When we say 70 percent, it really means 70 percent](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/when-we-say-70-percent-it-really-means-70-percent).
- The BoE's pre-1996 bands were set from the average absolute error of past forecasts, and the article states the expected hit rate ("the outturn ... within the blue area just over half of the time") (guidance): [BoE 1998](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1998/the-inflation-report-projections-understanding-the-fan-chart). The 2024 Bernanke review recommends a section on uncertainty including the forecast error record and analysis of recent errors (guidance): [BoE review, Recommendation 11](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/independent-evaluation-office/forecasting-for-monetary-policy-making-and-communication-at-the-bank-of-england-a-review/forecasting-for-monetary-policy-making-and-communication-at-the-bank-of-england-a-review).
- Forecast hubs take forecasts in real time and close submissions (the US COVID-19 hub stopped accepting submissions on 2024-05-01, keeping data public), which is what makes their records real-time (guidance): [US COVID-19 Forecast Hub README](https://github.com/reichlab/covid19-forecast-hub/blob/master/data-processed/README.md).
- hubVis can draw observed target data past the forecast origin over the forecast ribbons, the layout used to compare forecasts with what happened (guidance): [hubVis reference](https://hubverse-org.github.io/hubVis/reference/plot_step_ahead_model_output.html).
- Spiegelhalter et al.: "Acknowledge the limitations of the information conveyed in its quality and relevance" (guidance): [Science 2011](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/spiegelhalter_visualizing.pdf).

### Inferences
- aseri's spec already does the core things right: live vs retrospective flags, live-only headline record, a 30-forecast minimum, never combining backtest and live in one figure, a fixed rule for the record dots (last 8 weekly forecasts at 7 days ahead). These match the sources above.
- Sampling noise is large at the threshold: at a true 80% coverage and n = 30 independent forecasts, the standard error is sqrt(0.8 x 0.2 / 30) = 7.3 points, so roughly 66% to 94% is consistent with a well-calibrated model; overlapping origins and pooled horizons make the effective n smaller still. The spec's backtest range (72 to 87%) is inside this noise. The page should show "expected about 8 in 10; got 23 of 30" with that noise, not a bare percentage.
- Replay must show the observations as they were known on the forecast date as well as the current vintage for GPR (which revises), or at least say which vintage is shown; otherwise the replay is a mild hindsight display (Bracher and Funk; Croushore and Stark).
- Cherry-picking risk sits in defaults: the replay should open on today and the record dots on a fixed rule, never on a best-looking day; the summary "how many days landed inside" should cover all target days of the replayed forecast.
- Label words: "Backtest" (or "made after the fact") on every retrospective forecast in the replay, in the slider's track itself (e.g. a different tick colour/hatch for the backfill span), not only in the source line.

**Rules for the aseri chart, with checks**
1. Every retrospective forecast is labelled where it is drawn, not only in a footnote. Check: unit test that replaying a `forecast_mode: retrospective` forecast renders the "Backtest" label in the panel title or readout.
2. Coverage is shown as hits / n with the expected rate and an uncertainty range; never as a lone percentage. Check: snapshot of the record table; test that coverage text includes n.
3. Live and backtest never share a number, chart or average. Check: schema test on `scores.json` that each figure carries one `forecast_mode`; UI test that the backtest block renders under its own heading.
4. Scoring vintage is stated and fixed (spec 6.3); replay states which vintage the white dots are. Check: replay readout includes "data as of {date}".
5. Defaults are fixed rules: replay opens on today; dots use the fixed weekly rule. Check: e2e test of initial state.

### Gaps
- I found no visualization study on how lay readers interpret calibration plots or coverage counts; FiveThirtyEight's practice is guidance, not evidence of comprehension.
- The Bracher and Funk preprint is recent (Aug 2026) and was read at abstract level only.

## 6. Accessibility of forecast charts: text equivalents and colour contrast

### Takeaway
WCAG 2.x requires a text alternative for the canvas, 3:1 contrast for chart lines and areas needed to understand the chart (unless the same information is in text or labels), 4.5:1 for text, and no reliance on colour alone. aseri's text readout and intervals block cover the text-equivalent requirement; its token pairs fail where two marks must be told apart by colour (median vs 7-day line, 1.26:1) and on the frame colour (1.6:1) if gridlines or the hatch carry meaning.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (guidance, normative standard): "The lines should have 3:1 contrast against their background"; it covers "each line in a graph"; an exception applies when "A graphic with text embedded or overlaid conveys the same information, such as labels and values on a chart", and "Text within a graphic must meet 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum)"; for gradients or multiple colours "choose the least contrasting area to test": [W3C Understanding 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast).
- WCAG SC 1.4.1 Use of Color: colour must not be the only visual means of conveying information or distinguishing elements (guidance): [W3C Understanding 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html).
- WCAG SC 1.1.1 Non-text Content: non-text content needs a text alternative serving the equivalent purpose (guidance): [W3C Understanding 1.1.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-content.html).
- Text versions of predictive intervals were read correctly by 94% vs a 36% misreading with the graphic (empirical), so text equivalents are also a comprehension aid, not only an accessibility one: [Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full).
- Avoid warning colours (yellow, orange, red, violet) in uncertainty displays (guidance, Fundel et al. 2019 as cited): [Joslyn and Savelli 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.590232/full).

### Inferences
- Measured contrasts (computed here, see the top of this file): pass for gold, pale gold, label grey, inside green, outside red and text against the background; muted gold band pixels pass at 4.6:1 per pixel but the dithered area is lighter on average, so test the least contrasting area per W3C; frame #253a56 fails 3:1 (fine for decorative grid and hatch only if the forecast zone is also named in text, which it is: "Next N days"); focus blue #5b6ee1 at 4.2:1 passes 3:1 for the dotted origin line.
- Median (pale gold) vs 7-day average (gold) at 1.26:1 is a 1.4.1 problem: the two lines differ only by a slight hue/lightness shift, which is exactly why the eye sees one line continuing across the origin. A shape cue (dotted vs solid, thickness) is needed, independent of the quantity fix in section 2.
- Past daily dots (label grey) vs replayed real values (white) differ at 2.3:1, below 3:1; add a shape difference (filled vs hollow, or size) so "after the origin" does not rely on lightness alone.
- The canvas already has `role="img"` and an `aria-label` summary; the summary should state the quantity, the anchor value and date, the 8-in-10 range and median for the step days, matching the visible readout, and the same numbers should be reachable as a table (the intervals block) for screen readers.
- Gold on navy is a yellow; Fundel's warning-colour advice targets hazard maps and is weak evidence here, but the outside-range red (#d95763) should not be reused for anything but "outside".

**Rules for the aseri chart, with checks**
1. Contrast test in CI: compute WCAG ratios from the design tokens; assert >= 3:1 vs background for every meaning-bearing mark (daily marks, median, band edge, origin line) and >= 4.5:1 for label text; assert that any two marks that must be told apart either differ by >= 3:1 or differ in shape (documented in the test). Review: check the dithered band at its least contrasting area.
2. No colour-only distinctions: median vs average, past vs replayed values, inside vs outside dots each have a second cue (dash pattern, shape, or text). Check: grayscale screenshot review; the chart must still be readable.
3. Text equivalent equals the visible facts: the `aria-label` summary and the intervals block carry the same numbers as the chart, with the quantity named. Check: unit test building the summary from the same fixture and asserting it contains the anchor value, the quantity name and every step's range.
4. Reduced motion: spec 6.8 already disables scan and dither animations; keep the replay usable by keyboard (spec already has arrow keys and T). Check: existing a11y tests.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Chartability or other chart-specific accessibility audits; the rules rest on WCAG text only.
- I did not measure the rendered effective contrast of the dithered bands (it depends on display scaling); only token-level ratios were computed.
